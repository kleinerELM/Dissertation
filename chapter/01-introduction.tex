\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and motivation}

With a world-wide production of \qty{3.8}{\giga\tonne} in 2025 \cite{usgs.2026}, Portland cement is the most widely used binder for the production of concrete.
After adding water to the cement, a reaction called `hydration' begins.
The cement reactivity is determined by its composition and particle size distribution.
During hydration, the cement begins to dissolve and new hydration products start to form (see \zcref{fig:intro}).
This ultimately causes hardening of the cement paste and concrete.
Furthermore, this microstructure determines properties like strength and durability of the resulting concrete.

In 1929 \textsc{Anderegg} and \textsc{Hubbell} introduced their paper \cite{anderegg.1929} as follows:
\begin{iquote}
	The lack of knowledge concerning the rate of hydration of cement clinker has hindered a more complete understanding of the complicated setting and hardening reactions that cements undergo. 
\end{iquote}

While the knowledge and measurement techniques have significantly improved within the last century, this is still true to a certain extent today.
Good knowledge of the structural development and thus, reliable measurement and evaluation methods are crucial to understand, model \cite{nguyen-tuan.2023,nguyen-tuan.2024} and consequently influence the hydration of cements.
Good measurement and evaluation methods are therefore required.

Microscopy was employed early in cement research to understand the hardening process of cementitious materials \cite{anderegg.1929}.
Advances in optical and in particular in electron microscopy helped to clarify previous misconceptions and provided the means to influence the properties of concrete. 
For example, it has been proven that the `cement gel', which forms during hydration, actually consists of needle-shaped crystallites.
Later it was proven, that the hollow hydration shells which could be found in hydrated cements \cite{hadley.1972} were actually no preparation artefacts but a real phenomenon \cite{barnes.1978}.

A \ac{SEM} is a powerful tool to image the microstructure of a lot of materials.
Electron microscopy is widely known for its \ac{SE}-mode, which gives a helpful insights into the surface profile of the specimen.
The \ac{BSE}-mode is often used for polished samples resulting in a chemical contrast image.
Nevertheless, the data extracted from the resulting images can be greatly improved, if also data from \ac{EDX}, \ac{EBSD}, optical microscopy, \ac{mCT}, laser ablation or \ac{NI} are recorded.
The combination of these (imaging) techniques is often referred to as correlative microscopy.
It results in significantly more insights than the simple sum of the individual methods would be able to produce.

Modern computer vision methods allow the development of complicated and creative evaluation methods, which were computationally too expensive just a few years ago.
Therefore, existing analytical methods are to be improved and further developed to be reproducible and fairly easily repeatable.

This thesis is devoted to improve the characterisation of the microstructure of unhydrated and hydrated cement-based binders by combining a set of imaging techniques like \ac{SEM} and \ac{mCT}.
The results of these methods can then be correlated to selected methods like \ac{LTDSC} and \ac{MIP}.
The main goals are to better utilise methods and techniques, find improved ways to combine them to gain better knowledge about unhydrated and hydrated cements and the cement hydration, define protocols and best practices to obtain statistically relevant data, and to develop or adopt software tools for automated image analysis.
In this regard, the composition of clinker as well as the structure and porosity of hydration products are the focus of this work.

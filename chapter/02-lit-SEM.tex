\section{\Acl*{SEM} (\acs*{SEM}) for building material characterisation}

% imaging overview paper \cite{qoku.2023}
The first electron microscope was a constructed by \textsc{Ernst Ruska} and \textsc{Max Knoll} in 1931 \cite{knoll.1932} and was based on the transmission of electrons trough a thin specimen.
This class of microscopes is therefore called today \acs{TEM}.
It was first applied in 1939 by \textcite{rapczewski.1939} to image hydration products of \ac{C3S}.
In the 1950s and 1960s, electron microscopy in combination with electron diffraction developed to become a useful and reliable tool for the analysis of cement and its hydration products \cite{grudemo.1960, grudemo.1965}.
However, it had multiple drawbacks such as the requirement of very small (powdered) and thin specimens to be able to penetrate the specimen with the electron beam.

In 1937, \textsc{Manfred von Ardenne} built the first \ac{SEM} which enabled to also image the surface of specimens \cite{vonardenne.1938}, allowing for a wider range of preparation methods.
It took until 1965, when the first commercial \ac{SEM} made by \textsc{Cambridge Instrument Co.} was available.
Only a year later, \textcite{chatterji.1966} used this \ac{SEM} to observe fractured specimens of hydrated cement.
They already observed the formation of the needle-like structure of \ac{CSH}.
Today, \ac{SEM} is a widely used tool for the analysis of building materials.
Modern \ac{SEM}s are equipped with various detectors, like \ac{SE}, \ac{BSE}, \ac{EDX} and \ac{EBSD} detectors.
They allow to observe the chemical composition, the crystallographic orientation and the topography of the specimen.

\subsection{\Acl*{SE} (\acs*{SE}) imaging}
\label{sec:se}

\ac{SE} detectors primarily capture low-energy secondary electrons (<\,\SI{50}{\electronvolt}) which are inelastically scattered and emitted from the very near-surface region (a depth of <\,\SI{50}{\nano\meter}) of the sample \cite{pawley.1984}. %fylak.2011
Therefore, their detection is highly sensitive to the surface topography of the sample.
Edges, and surfaces tilted towards the detector emit more electrons than surfaces facing away from the detector.

Especially improvements in low-\si{\kilo\volt} \ac{SE} imaging significantly improved the resolution, contrast and topographic representation of specimens \cite{pawley.1984,moser.1998}.
This in contrast to the relatively long electron wavelength of  \SI{38.8}{\pico\meter} at \SI{1}{\kilo\volt} compared to \SI{12.2}{\pico\meter} at \SI{10}{\kilo\volt}.
The improvement is instead caused by the reduced penetration depth of the electrons, less pronounced charging artefacts and reduced beam damage \cite{pawley.1984}.
The voltage reduction also allows to omit conductive coatings \cite{pawley.1984,moser.1996}.
While the advantages of low-\si{\kilo\volt} imaging were known early on \cite{vonardenne.1940}, it took until the late 70s and early 80s until problems like chromatic aberration and low source brightness were reduced in the first widely available \ac{SEM}s equipped with a field emission gun and more sensitive detectors were developed \cite{pawley.1984,moser.1996}.

Current \ac{SEM}s are able to further improve the \ac{SE}-imaging utilising voltages of a few hundred Volts and beam deceleration techniques, allowing for high resolutions of uncoated specimens in the \si{\nano\meter} scale.

\subsection{\Acl*{BSE} (\acs*{BSE}) detector}
\label{sec:bse}

\ac{BSE} detectors collect high-energy electrons from the primary beam that have been elastically scattered back out of the sample after interacting with atomic nuclei.
Heavier elements (with a higher atomic number $Z$) scatter electrons more strongly and appear brighter in the \ac{BSE} image.
The higher the voltage and the lower the material density, the larger the interaction volume and depth (see Figure~\ref{fig:penetration_depth}).

In a polished cross-section of hardened cement paste, \ac{BSE} imaging allows therefore the differentiation of phases: unhydrated clinker particles (higher average $Z$ ($\bar{Z}$)) appear bright, hydrates (intermediate $\bar{Z}$) appears as a grey phase, and porosity (e.g. filled with epoxy resin) appears darkest (black).

However, especially hydrate phases like calcite, \ac{CH}, ettringite, \ac{CSH} and \ac{AFm} can in some cases be difficult to distinguish from each other.
This is caused by a low difference in the backscatter coefficient ($\eta$) of these phases.
This coefficient can be directly calculated from the atomic number $Z$ of the elements in the material \cite{reuter.1972,goldstein.1992a}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bse_eta}
    \eta = -0.0254 \cdot 0.016Z - 1.86 \cdot 10^{-4}Z^2 + 8.3 \cdot 10^{-7}Z^3
\end{equation}

For materials composed from multiple elements, the backscatter coefficient can be calculated as a weighted sum of the individual backscatter coefficients:
\begin{equation}
    \eta = \sum_{i} c_i \eta_i
\end{equation}
, where $c_i$ is the concentration of the element $i$ and $\eta_i$ is its backscatter coefficient.

The contrast $C$ can be calculated as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:bse_contrast} from the the backscatter coefficient ($\eta$) of a material (\cite{goldstein.1992}, Equation~4.11), where $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$ are the backscatter coefficients of the two materials:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bse_contrast}
    C = \frac{\lvert \eta_2 - \eta_1 \rvert}{\eta_2}
\end{equation}

The backscatter coefficient is defined as the ratio of the number of backscattered electrons to the number of incident electrons \cite{goldstein.1992a}
Some selected phases and their respective $\eta$ are listed in Table~\ref{tab:bse_eta_Z}.

\subsection{\Acl*{EDX} (\acs*{EDX})}
\label{sec:edx}

\ac{EDX} is a widely used technology available even on smaller \ac{SEM}-devices.
It is based on the interaction of the electron beam with the atoms in the sample surface.
When the high-energy beam electrons strike the sample, they can knock out inner-shell electrons from the sample atoms.
The outer-shell electrons will fall back to the energy level of the knocked out electrons, releasing the energy difference as X-rays.
The energies of these X-ray are unique fingerprints of specific elements.
An \ac{EDX} detector measures the energy and intensity (counts) of the emitted X-rays.
This allows for qualitative identification and semi-quantitative or quantitative analysis.
Point analyses or elemental maps can show the spatial distribution of these elements and thus the associated phases.

The excitation voltage has to be about \numrange{2}{3} times as high as the expected X-ray energies of the elements of interest to collect enough counts to be able identify and quantify the concentration of the elements.% \acs{Ka}  or \acs{Kb}
This results in a minimal required electron beam voltage of \SI{10}{\kilo\volt} to measure e.g. \ce{Ca} with a \ac{Ka} of \SI{3.69}{\kilo\electronvolt} \cite{hudson.2003}.
Using low voltages will result in lower X-ray-counts, which can only be countered by using a very sensitive detector, which has a large detection area and/or has no window \cite{yamamoto.2016}.
This can also enable to detect light elements like \ce{Li}, \ce{Be} and \ce{B} \cite{yamamoto.2016}.
However, these elements still are challenging to differentiate in most specimens with a more complex composition due to the overlap of X-ray energies \cite{hudson.2003}.

The lateral resolution of the \ac{EDX} analysis is limited by the electron beam voltage used.
Similar to the \ac{BSE}, the higher the voltage, the larger the interaction volume and depth (see Figure~\ref{fig:penetration_depth}), and therefore the larger the area from which X-rays are emitted \cite{rossen.2017}.
Depending on the beam voltage required, the interaction volume and therefore the resolution can be below \SI{1}{\micro\meter} \cite{jiang.2022}.
Furthermore, a larger interaction volume will cause subsurface information being included in the measured spectra.

\subsection{\Acl*{EBSD} (\acs*{EBSD})}
\label{sec:ebsd}

\ac{EBSD} is a technique used in the \ac{SEM} to determine the crystallographic structure and orientation of crystalline materials in the sample.
The electron beam interacts with a highly tilted crystalline sample, and some electrons are diffracted according to \textsc{Bragg}'s Law \cite{bragg.1913} by the crystal lattice planes.
These diffracted electrons form \textcite{kikuchi.1928} patterns on a phosphor screen, which are captured by a camera.
By indexing these patterns, \ac{EBSD} can be used to identify the crystal (e.g., distinguish between \ac{C3S} and \ac{C2S}) as pioneered in 1954 by \textcite{alam.1954}.
This allows to determine the precise crystallographic orientation at that point on the sample.
Scanning across the sample generates maps of phase distribution, crystal orientation, grain boundaries, and texture.

The term \ac{EBSD} was introduced in 1973 by \textcite{venables.1973}.
The first commercial \ac{EBSD} system was introduced in 1984 by \textsc{Moon} and \textsc{Harris} based on the work of \textsc{Dingley} \cite{wright.2000} and has since become a standard tool in materials science.
Often it is used simultaneously with \ac{EDX} to provide a comprehensive analysis of the sample \cite{wright.2000}.

\subsection{\Acl*{FIB} (\acs*{FIB})}
\label{sec:lit_fib}

Besides the classical detectors, modern \ac{SEM}s can also be equipped with a \ac{FIB}.
Instead of electrons, ions (usually \ce{Ga+}) are accelerated to the specimen surface.
\ac{FIB} systems enable the targeted removal of material or to deposit coatings (e.g. \ce{Pt} or \ce{C}) to protect a \ac{ROI}, to weld  the specimen to a nearby object or to make a surface conductive.

In 1974/75 the \ac{FIB} technique was introduced by \textcite{levi-setti.1974} and \textcite{orloff.1975} for microprobing applications.
\ac{FIB} systems are broadly available since the 1990s \cite{volkert.2007}.
While it is also possible to obtain an image of the specimen surface with a resolution of $\approx$\SI{5}{\nano\meter} \cite{orloff.1996}, the main application is the preparation of samples for \ac{SEM} and \ac{TEM} due to the abrading effect of the ions.
For example, \textcite{sakalli.2017} used a \ac{FIB} to prepare a \ac{TEM} lamella of hydrated \ac{C3S} and analysed it using \ac{EDX}. % The hydrate seems to be damaged
While the first \ac{FIB}s were single beam devices, today combined \ac{FIB}-\ac{SEM} devices are the norm.

The \ac{ROI} has to be in the eucentric point (point of rotation/intersection point of the electron and ion beam).
This means, if the specimen is tilted, the point does not move in the live image and therefore is in the rotational center.

These capabilities, allow to capture three-dimensional datasets using one or a combination of the detectors available (e.g. \ac{BSE}, \ac{EDX}, \ac{EBSD}).
In contrast to X-ray \ac{CT}, this method is intrinsically destructive.

\subsection{Cryo-\acs*{FIB} and cryo-\acs*{FIB}}
\label{sec:lit_cryo}

Cryo-preparation for \acs*{SEM} and \acs*{FIBnt} is a method to obtain close-to-nature high-resolution images of water containing specimens \cite{desbois.2008}.
It works by rapidly freezing the specimen in liquid nitrogen within a high-pressure freezer, which prevents the formation of ice crystals and preserves the original structure of the sample \cite{holzer.2007,muller.2021}.
This method is often used in the biological field to obtain 3D datasets of cells or tissues \cite{muller.2021}.

\subsection{Sample preparation - mechanical polishing}
\label{sec:lit_sample_preparation}

%\end{figure}

Detectors such as \ac{BSE} and \ac{EDX} provide the best results with the smoothest possible surfaces of sample cross-sections.
Thus, resin embedding and mechanical polishing are very common methods to polish samples \cite{scrivener.2004}.
The sample is embedded in a resin (e.g. epoxy resin) to stabilize the specimen and to fill the pores.
Then, the sample is polished using a series of oil-based polishing slurries with decreasing grain size (see Section~\ref{sec:mech_pol}).
However, it is difficult to obtain a surface without scratches using mechanical polishing (see Figure~\ref{fig:mech_polishing_damage}).

Due to hardness differences between the phases, the polishing process can also cause uneven surfaces in the specimen \cite{ji.2014}.
This is especially true for resin embedded specimens \cite{desbois.2010}.
Furthermore, smearing of the softer material (e.g. resin) can occur, filling pores or influencing subsequent analysis like \ac{EDX}.
All these influences can lead to a significant reduction in image quality and resolution.
\textcite{scrivener.2004} achieved a feature resolution of \ac{CSH}, ettringite, and pores of \SIrange{0.1}{0.2}{\micro\meter} using mechanical polishing techniques.
This feature resolution is not sufficient for the analysis of nanoscale structures.
\ac{CSH} needles, for example, can have a diameter significantly less than \SI{100}{\nano\meter} \cite{moser.2003} and therefore cannot be acceptably imaged.
While mechanical polishing still can be good enough for many applications like \ac{BSE}- or \ac{EDX}-imaging, high-resolution imaging or \ac{EBSD} require better surface qualities without contaminations \cite{rossler.2017}.

\subsection{Sample preparation - \Acl*{ArBIB} polishing}

\Ac{ArBIB} sectioning is an alternative method to polish specimen surfaces \cite{harper.1983,grunewald.1987}.
In a broad ion beam device, accelerated ions (e.g. \ce{Ar+}) are used to clean and polish the specimen surface.
So prepared surfaces show limited damage to the nano-scale features of interest \cite{desbois.2009,desbois.2010},
Furthermore, small pores are not filled with debris \cite{desbois.2009,desbois.2010}, which eliminates the need for resin intrusion \cite{desbois.2009}, while it is still possible to do so \cite{rossler.2017} and advisable for later segmentation purposes.

\Ac{ArBIB} allows to polish large areas in the range of several \si{\milli\meter\squared} \cite{hauffe.2003,desbois.2013}.
Therefore, some authors utilised the broad ion beam polishing as a tool for tomography \cite{desbois.2013,mac.2018}.
Compared to \ac{FIBnt}, the slice thickness of this technique is large and inconsistent (>\,\SI{350(70)}{\nano\meter} compared to \SIrange{5}{10}{\nano\meter}) and the effort required is very high \cite{desbois.2013}.

Hydrate phases can easily be damaged by high temperatures.
Thermal damage is especially known for phases like \ac{CSH}, ettringite and \ac{CH} \cite{lothenbach.2016}.
%The water content of \ac{CSH} changes depending on the temperature \textcite{sanna.2014}.
For example, \ac{CSH} begins to dehydrate at \SI{60}{\celsius} \cite{he.2018} and the water saturation and the size of gel pores change at this temperature \cite{gajewicz.2016}.
Ettringite can be dehydrated below \SI{100}{\celsius} (Figure~\ref{fig:ettringite_dehydration}).


After prolonged electron exposure, especially at higher beam voltages, alterations of sensitive specimens can be observed.
These artefacts can be attributed to the electron beam heating the specimen or to the formation of gaseous products attributed to radiation damage.
However, if thermal damage occurs is highly depended on the specimen (thermal conductivity, melting or decomposition temperature) and the applied scanning parameters (scanned area, beam voltage and current, dwell time). \cite{reimer.1998b}

For cement hydrates, damages can be observed for example after a prolonged \ac{EDX}-measurement using a high-powered electron beam.
Also, at very high magnifications, the electron dose is very high even at low beam voltages and currents.
In a literature review from 2002 \textcite{gartner.2002} pointed to a lot of studies of morphologically damaged \ac{CSH} phases in \ac{TEM} and \ac{SEM} images.
The damages were mostly caused by the preparation and beam damage \cite{gartner.2002}.
The difference in preparation methods was also demonstrated by \textcite{moser.1998,moser.2003} using an environmental \ac{SEM} (\acs{ESEM}).
\textcite{rossler.2006} were able to show, that $\approx$ \SI{8e2}{\elementarycharge\per\square\angstrom}  at \SI{-172}{\degreeCelsius} allow to retrieve electron diffraction patterns and artefact-free imaging \ac{CSH}, while \SI{6.2e3}{\elementarycharge\per\square\angstrom} at room temperature induce significant beam damage to the mineral, amorphising it in the process.
A high energy dose in the \ac{SEM} can cause geometrical deformation of \ac{CSH}-structures and introduce pores within originally compact phases (see Figure~\ref{fig:ebeam_damage}) \cite{moser.2003,kleiner.2021a}.

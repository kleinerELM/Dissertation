These reaction products are responsible for the hardening of cement-based materials.
The main reaction, forming \ac{CSH} and \ac{CH}, can be summarised as follows \cite{gartner.2002}:

\reaction[eq:CSHn]{C3S + (3 - $x$ + $n$)H -> C_{x}SH_{n} + (3-$x$)CH}
\reaction[eq:CSH1.7]{C2S + 4.3H -> C_{1.7}SH4 + 0.3CH }

The hydration process can be subdivided into five phases as indicated in \zcref{fig:opc_dca}.

As described in \cite{gartner.2002}, the phases are:
\begin{multicols}{2}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item [0] Initial fast reaction
    \item [I] First deceleration period
    \item [II] Induction phase
    \item [III] Acceleration phase
    \item [IV] Second deceleration period
    \item [V] Final slow reaction
  \end{itemize}
\end{multicols}

The hydration process within the first 24 hours, particularly up to the end of the main hydration peak (phase III), is crucial for describing the initial hardening process and the rheological behaviour of the cement.
The first useful qualitative observations at room temperature were made using the \ac{SE} mode and freshly fractured surfaces after a few minutes or up to \qty{10}{\hour} \cite{stark.2001,voland.2000,suss.2001}.
At this time, \ac{CSH} needles are up to \qty{300}{\nano\meter} long, while the hydration front is barely visible in the particle \cite{voland.2000}.
In contrast, reliable quantitative observations on embedded and polished surfaces using the \ac{BSE} mode can typically only be made after the initial setting, which takes a few hours.
Before this point, the material is not sufficiently hardened to withstand the preparation process without disintegrating into a powder.
While \textcite{neubauer.2022} performed \ac{BSE} analysis on dispersed \ac{C3S} particles after \qty{12}{\hour}, detailed studies on polished sections of cement paste at very early ages (i.e. within the first hours) are scarce.
This is a significant challenge, as such data would be valuable for validating hydration models like those developed by \textcite{nguyen-tuan.2024}.

\section{Growth of \acl*{CSH} (\acs*{CSH})}

\ac{CSH} is the decisive phase for the strength of concrete during the later hydration process.
Detailed knowledge of \ac{CSH} development in volume as well as microstructural shape is especially important for simulating its growth \cite{breugel.1997,nguyen-tuan.2023,nguyen-tuan.2024} and the resulting pore space.

\subsection{\acs*{CSH} morphology and structure}

The ideal \ce{CaO}:\ce{SiO2} ratio of \num{1.7} is not always attained because \ac{CSH} commonly contains numerous crystallographic defects. 
Consequently, a variety of morphologies is observed. 
For example, \ac{TEM} investigations by \textcite{melzer.1989} identified five distinct variants of \ac{CSH}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item `gel-like' \ac{CSH}: vermicular-like; surrounding anhydrous \ac{C3S} 
    \item $\alpha$-\ac{CSH}: type 1 `needle-like', type 2 `sword-like' (more crystalline); projecting radially from the `gel-like' \ac{CSH}
    \item $\beta$-\ac{CSH}: `sheet-like' particles; often conglomerated; also epitaxial intergrowth with $\gamma$- and $\delta$-\ac{CSH} and \ac{CH}
    \item $\gamma$-\ac{CSH}: epitaxial growths with $\beta$-\ac{CSH} and \ac{CH}
    \item $\delta$-\ac{CSH}: epitaxial growths with $\beta$- and $\gamma$-\ac{CSH} and \ac{CH}
\end{itemize}

However, this classification is not often used in the literature (e.g. \cite{nonat.2004,haas.2015}).

Their definition and terms vary between publications and may sometimes not mean exactly the same phenomenon:
\begin{itemize}
    \item inner / outer product \cite{scrivener.1986, richardson.1993}

In this document they will be called \ac{CSHi} for the dense inner structure and \ac{CSHo} for the needle- or foil-like outer structure.


The needle-like structure was first described by \textcite{lechatelier.1887} in 1887, including its estimated chemical composition.

\subsection{\acs*{CSH} – gel or crystal?}

In early publications \cite{lea.1937,powers.1958}, the hydration products of cement were often referred to as `cement gel'.
According to \textsc{IUPAC}, a gel is defined as:
\begin{iquote}
    Non-fluid colloidal network or polymer network that is expanded throughout its whole volume by a fluid. \cite{gold.2019}
\end{iquote}

This early attribution was mainly due to the physical properties of fresh cement paste \cite{lea.1937}.
For example, \textcite{baykoff.1926} suggested that the colloidal nature only exists between dissolution and final crystallisation due to a phenomenon known today as \textcite{ostwald.1897} ripening.
However, in recent literature, the \ac{CSH} phase is still often referred to as `gel' \cite{masoero.2018, walkley.2019, duque-redondo.2022}. %hou.2015,

In the literature, two main reasons can be found for the classification of \ac{CSH} as gel:

Firstly, \ac{CSH} is often referred to as a `gel' due to its X-ray amorphous nature compared to crystalline \ac{CSH} or tobermorite \cite{jennings.1981, garbev.2004}.
So one may argue that water bound within the layers of the \ac{CSH} is the fluid throughout the unordered `\ac{CSH}-network' instead of bound crystal water.
However, it was shown by electron diffraction in (cryo-)\ac{TEM} that mature \ac{CSH} phases are (crypto-)crystalline \cite{kurczyk.1960, grudemo.1960, grudemo.1965, melzer.1989, rossler.2006} with a similar structure to the tobermorite group \cite{grudemo.1960,kurczyk.1960,biagioni.2015}.
Also, \textcite{nonat.2004} demonstrated the crystalline nature of \ac{CSH} crystallised on a calcite single crystal using atomic force microscopy.
However, during its very early development, \ac{CSH} forms water-rich droplets, which then aggregate on top of \ac{C3S} and \ac{C2S} surfaces in an amorphous state \cite{sowoidnich.2023}.
\textcite{bellmann.2012} claimed that then a thin \qty{2}{\nano\meter} layer is formed, covering the \ac{C3S} grains.
The forming of this amorphous first layer could be understood as a `gel'.
According to \textcite{jennings.1986a} and others \cite{bellmann.2015, naber.2019}, this layer can also act as a passivating layer causing the induction periods.

However, a gel can also be defined as a substance capable of swelling in the presence of a second component (in this case water) \cite{hermans.1946}.
This seems to be the case, as evident in the creep, swelling, and shrinkage behaviour of concrete \cite{powers.1958,feldman.1970}, which can be attributed to \ac{CSH} \cite{zhou.2025}.

The colloidal nature of hydration products of cement was proposed more than a century ago, as a lively discussion between \textcite{michaelis.1908} and the editorial of the \textsc{Tonindustrie-Zeitung} in 1907/08 demonstrates.
However, \textcite{chatelier.1918} disputed the use of the term colloid in cement hardening in 1918.
The required colloidal nature – i.e. a porous structure between \qty{1}{\nano\meter} and \qty{1}{\micro\meter} – can be applied to water-saturated, low-density \ac{CSHo} and very young \ac{CSH} \cite{sowoidnich.2023}.
However, dense \ac{CSHi} as observed in \ac{SEM} images, has a very low porosity and its morphology can hardly be classified as colloidal.
Therefore, the swelling is often attributed to the interlayer porosity of \ac{CSH} \cite{jennings.2008}, which typically ranges from \qtyrange{0.9}{1.4}{\nano\meter} for tobermorite-like structures.
According to \textcite{duque-redondo.2022}, a porosity of \qty{1.4}{\nano\meter} seems to be more fitting for \ac{CSH}.
At this pore size, the colloidal criteria for a gel would be fulfilled.

In 1937, \textcite{lea.1937} called hardened cement a `rigid gel' and described similarities to silica gel, which is today classified as a `xerogel' (a solid dried gel).
Accordingly, \textcite{wittmann.1976,wittmann.1977} described \ac{CSH} phases as xerogel in the 1970s.
However, some authors like \textcite{almdal.1993} argue that `\textit{xerogel and undiluted systems should not be admitted as gels}'.
Furthermore, they discussed that a gel should contain a `\textit{substantial quantity}' of liquid \cite{almdal.1993}.
This is debatable for \ac{CSH}, since a lot of water is bound in its crystalline structure.

In 2001, \textcite{stark.2001} concluded that while the designation `\ac{CSH}-gel' is correct `\textit{from a physical point of view as gel implies a particle size}', `\textit{from the point of view of cement chemistry and mineralogy}' \ac{CSH} cannot be classified as gel due to its nano-crystalline nature.

Considering the literature, the term `\ac{CSH}-gel' will not be used in this work due to the nano-crystalline nature of \ac{CSH}.
While \ac{CSH} forms a colloidal network, it forms a rigid solid, not the soft, elastic material often associated with the common understanding of a gelatin-like `gel'.
However, since the term `gel pores', describing the pores within the \ac{CSH} phases, is generally used term without any good replacement in terms of its meaning, it will still be used in this work.

\textcite{garrault.2006} dissolved \qty{4}{\gram} \ac{C3S} grains with different median diameters (7, 10, 11, 12.5, \qty{14}{\micro\meter}) for up to one day in a concentrated lime solution and measured the development of the resulting \ac{CSH} concentration.
They estimated the thickness of \ac{CSHi} (replacing \ac{C3S}) and \ac{CSHo} (on top of the grain) at the time, when the \ac{CSH} precipitation became limited by diffusion (see \zcref{fig:garrault-distribution}) (beginning of the second deceleration period in \zcref{fig:opc_dca}).
For a particle diameter of \qty{7}{\micro\meter} the diffusion was limited after $\approx$ \qty{600}{\min} (\qty{10}{\hour}), for \qty{14}{\micro\meter} after $\approx$ \qty{700}{\min} (\qty{12}{\hour}).

The estimation is based on the \ac{CSH}-concentration (the measurement method was not mentioned), the mean diameter and that the particles are spherical.
\textcite{garrault.2006} included a measurement of a single \ac{TEM}-image from  of a hydrated \ac{C3S} grain with a diameter of \qty{3}{\micro\meter}. % made by \textsc{Lachowski} and \textsc{Courault} in 1998.

The data of \textcite{garrault.2006} indicate a smaller \ac{HLT} with increasing mean particle diameters.
This is contradicting the data of \textsc{Anderegg} and \textsc{Hubbell} \cite{anderegg.1929,anderegg.1930} (see \zcref{fig:anderegg-hubbel-distribution}).
However, \textsc{Anderegg} and \textsc{Hubbell} began their measurements after \qty{7}{\day} and therefore after the diffusion limited growth of the \ac{CSH}.
Furthermore, their mean particle diameter was significantly larger (starting from \qty{15.5}{\micro\meter}).

In the 1960s, \textcite{kurczyk.1960} used an electron microscope to measure the length of \ac{CSH}-needles of one-month hydrated \ac{C3S}.
They measured an average length of \qty{500}{\nano\meter}.
However, the hydrated material was ground and dispersed using ultrasonic treatment, which may have damaged the hydrates.

\textcite{nicoleau.2011} measured the \ac{CSH}-thickness using Cryo-\ac{SEM} on cracked surfaces of hydrated \ac{C3S} (\ac{wb} of \num{0.5}, $\vartheta=$\qty{20}{\celsius}) (see \zcref{fig:anderegg-hubbel}).
\textcite{bishnoi.2012} criticised this approach due to the rather uncontrolled cracking, the subjectivity of the measurement, and the occurrence of the sectioning effect, which was described in detail by \textcite{scrivener.2004}.
This effect is an issue for all 2D images of polished sections (e.g., typical \ac{SEM}-\ac{BSE} images) and will be described and discussed in Subsection~\zcref{subsec:sectioning-effect}.

\textcite{neubauer.2022} measured the same specimen using a near-field \ac{PXCT} at different hydration times.
Furthermore, they manually measured the thickness of \ac{CSHi} on \ac{C3S}, dispersed in epoxy resin utilizing \ac{SEM}-images.
They determined the thickness at \num{12}, \num{16}, and \qty{20}{\hour} as \qty{300(80)}{\nano\meter}, \qty{670(170)}{\nano\meter}, and \qty{800(260)}{\nano\meter}, respectively.
% TODO: <- somehow measure the thickness from the PXCT data?

\textcite{voland.2000} and \textcite{suss.2001} manually measured the development of the length of \ac{CSH}-needles (roughly equivalent to \ac{CSHo}) using \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE} images up to a specimen age of \qty{28}{\day}.
\textsc{Stark} and \textsc{Möser} et al. \cite{stark.2001,moser.2001,moser.2002} continued this work up to nearly three years.
These experiments were conducted using \ac{C3S} and $\beta$-\ac{C2S} at different temperatures.
They measured a minimum and maximum value, from which the mean was calculated.
However, the authors did not clarify how many measurements were taken for each data point.
Therefore, it should be assumed that these data are based on individual measurements.
The results are plotted in \zcref{fig:suess_CSH_C3S} (\ac{C3S}) and \zcref{fig:suess_CSH_C2S} ($\beta$-\ac{C2S}).

The formation of \ac{CSH} begins earlier at increasing temperatures.
This also causes a significant increase in the overall fibre length.
At the same temperature, \ac{C2S} demonstrates a slower \ac{CSH} growth.
The \ac{CSH} growth of \ac{C3S} (\ac{wb}=\num{0.5}) stagnates after 5 months according to \textcite{voland.2000}, \textcite{suss.2001}, \textsc{Stark} and \textsc{Möser} et al. \cite{stark.2001,moser.2002}.
\textcite{bazzoni.2014} observed this stagnation already after a day, depending on the \ac{wb} value.
A higher \ac{wb} value (\num{0.8} compared to \num{0.4}) led to longer \ac{CSH} needles.

In contrast, \textcite{moser.2002} indicates that the needle length of \ac{CSH} on \ac{C2S} (\ac{wb}=\num{0.5}) is still increasing significantly, even up to an age of \num{3} years.

\end{iquote}

These fibres grow in length during the course of hydration with hardly any changes in thickness. When growing unhindered, the needle-like \ac{CSH} phases can reach a length of approx. 1.4 µm. These applies only for hydration of alite (in pure belite: \ac{CSH}- length up to 2.5 µm). In compact concrete it is to be expected that there is intergrowth between the fibers as well as and interlocking with one another and thus gel pores are formed which have often been described.

However, because \ac{CSH} growth is typically impeded in real systems, it should be expected, that the needle-like phases are generally shorter than reported in the mentioned literature.

\subsection{Hadley grains}
\label{subsec:hadley}

Sometimes, in polished sections of hydrated cementitious materials, hollow hydrate shells, occasionally filled with an unhydrated clinker grain, can be observed (see \zcref{fig:hadley}).
These particles are so-called `\textcite{hadley.1972} grains' termed after \textsc{Hadley}, who first described this phenomenon in detail \cite{hadley.1972, hadley.2000}.
\textcite{kjellsen.1997} described the formation of hollow shells depending on the \ac{wb} value and silica fume content.
They observed an increase in porosity attributed to the \textsc{Hadley} grains with an increasing \ac{wb} value, especially for young specimens.
\ac{PXCT} enabled \textcite{shirani.2023} to analyse the early development of \textsc{Hadley} grains around \ac{C3S} grains.
They identified a gap of \qty{450}{\nano\meter} between \ac{CSH} and the unhydrated \ac{C3S}.
This \ac{CSH} had a very low density compared to more mature material.

\textcite{yio.2015a} observed the development of such a grain using confocal microscopy.
After \qty{28}{\day}, they observed `\textit{needle-like hydration products that connect the outer products and the anhydrous core}' \cite{yio.2015a}.
In the later stages, the grain can completely dissolve and empty shells of \ac{CSH} remain.
This can be seen in \zcref{fig:hadley}, where both filled and hollow \textsc{Hadley} grains are visible, especially around \ac{C2S}.

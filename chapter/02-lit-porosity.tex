\section{Pore analysis}
\label{sec:pore_analysis}

% pore analysis paper \cite{yio.2019,levitz.2007,muench.2008,desbois.2016...}

On initial thought, the measurement of pores and pore sizes seems to be fairly easy.
However, while air voids in mortar and concrete are mostly spherical due to the surface tension of the fresh binder, this significantly changes below a scale of tens of micrometers, where the capillary pores begin.
These pores are filled with pore solution/water during hydration and feature much more complex, fractal-like geometries.

There are a lot of methods to measure the \ac{PSD} of a hardened specimen.
However, each method has different measurement ranges as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pore_sizes}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering

	\includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{methods/pore_sizes.pdf}
    \caption{Phase and pore sizes compared to the measurement ranges of selected methods. Redrawn and expanded according to a figure by \textcite{stark.2000}, based on data from \textcite{romberg.1978}.}
    \label{fig:pore_sizes}
\end{figure}

Image based methods like the \ac{SEM} or the \ac{CT} have already been introduced above.
However, they inherently have either resolution issues, only give a 2-dimensional view or do not give representative results due to a small \ac{ROI}.
Therefore, more indirect methods based on physical principles can help to obtain more statistically significant results.
For this thesis, \ac{MIP}, \ac{LTDSC}, \ac{HNMR} were used as complementary methods to the image based methods.

\subsection{Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (\acs*{MIP})}
\label{sec:mip}
% \cite{munch.2008} FIB vs MIP!!! (11.9 x10.7 x5.6 µm)
%https://www.epfl.ch/labs/lmc/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/03-Wioletta-Franco-MIP.pdf
\ac{MIP} is a rather old and well known method to measure the \ac{PSD} of porous materials \cite{romberg.1978}.
According to \textcite{muller.2014} the \ac{MIP} technique allows to adequately quantify the capillary porosity of hydrated cement as long as it is dried properly using the solvent exchange method.
However, it is unreliable to extract the porosity of \ac{CSH} as it overestimates the porosity up to a factor of \num{3} to \num{4} \cite{muller.2014}.
\ac{MIP} is also deemed inappropriate by \textcite{diamond.2000} to measure the \ac{PSD} using of hydrated cement, since it `\textit{systematically misallocates the sizes of almost all of the volume of pores}' which is agreed upon by others \cite{chatterji.2001,giesche.2002}.
The `ink-bottle' effect (see Figure~\ref{fig:mip-bottle-neck}) is often mentioned as reason for the misallocation.

\textcite{wang.2019} showed the damaged induced by \ac{MIP} using \ac{mCT}.
They came to the conclusion, that the pore space was increased by approximately \SI{7}{\percent} due to the mercury intrusion.
The main affected pore size range was around 100-\SI{500}{\micro\meter}.
Furthermore, \ac{MIP} requires dried specimens, leading to a cracked and therefore non-native microstructure \cite{chatterji.2001,wang.2019}.

\textcite{abell.1999} and \textcite{hildenbrand.2003} compared \ac{MIP} with the intrusion of \textsc{Wood}'s metal instead of mercury.
In cement, the pore volume was comparable, the usage of \textsc{Wood}'s metal indicated that larger pores were present \cite{abell.1999}.
\textcite{hildenbrand.2003} applied this comparison to mudstones and concluded, that \ac{MIP} causes a significant compression of the sample pore space.
This was already assumed by \cite{horseman.1996} who declared \c{MIP} as `totally unreliable' for clay-like materials.
\textcite{hall.1986} concludes in comparison with small angle neutron scattering and \ce{N2} measurements, that pores than \SI{70}{\nano\meter} in clays larger may be preparation artifacts (e.g. due to drying).
\ac{MIP} was also deemed inadequate also for oolithic limestone by \textcite{roels.2001} due to the ink-bottle effect.
They preferred the main wetting curve, \ac{SEM} images and pressure membrane apparatus measurements of saturated specimens over \ac{MIP}.

Finally, due to the use of hazardous mercury and due to the high cost, it has a reduced relevance today.
Despite its flaws, \ac{MIP} is useful to measure the porosity of similar samples or the change in porosity within a specimen \cite{juntgen.1966}.

\subsection{Low Temperature - Differential Scanning Calorimetry (\acs{LTDSC})}
\label{sec:ltdsc}

Another less common method to indirectly measure the \ac{PSD}, especially in the nano-pore range, is the \ac{LTDSC} \cite{brun.1973,brun.1977}, often also called \textit{thermoporometry} (e.g. \cite{joseph.2024}).
The method is based on the principle, that the freezing point of water is lowered in small pores due to surface tension \cite{brun.1973,brun.1977}.
Since the heat capacity of the phase transition (liquid-solid) is significantly higher than that of the solid phases, this freezing point depression can be measured using \ac{DSC}.

The measurable pore size range starts at approx. \num{2} \cite{cuperus.1992} or \SI{3.19}{\nano\meter} due to a temperature limit in the thermodynamic description of \SI{-40}{\degreeCelsius} \cite{brun.1977}.
However, this limit can be extended to lower temperatures and smaller pores when considering the extrapolated curve for the triple point depression in porous materials \cite{kloetstra.1995}.
For pores larger than \SI{30}{\nano\meter} the temperature shift of the freezing point is too small to be separated from the freezing of free water \cite{cuperus.1992,stocker.2002}.

\ac{LTDSC} requires water-saturated specimens and therefore avoids damage due to drying \cite{gran.1997,galle.2001} often criticised in \ac{MIP} and \ac{SEM} experiments \cite{chatterji.2001,georget.2024}.
\textcite{sant.2011} applied \ac{LTDSC} to measure the \ac{PSD} of hydrated cement pastes.
They proposed the connection between pore types and the freezing point depression (ranges in brackets are extracted from graphs in \cite{sant.2011}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Connected capillary pores freeze at $\approx$ \SI{-15}{\degreeCelsius} (\num{0} to \SI{-23}{\degreeCelsius})
    \item Open gel pores and capillary pores at $\approx$ \SI{-27}{\degreeCelsius} (\num{-23} to \SI{-35}{\degreeCelsius})
    \item Isolated capillary and gel pores at $\approx$ \SI{-43}{\degreeCelsius} (\num{-35} to \SI{-50}{\degreeCelsius})
\end{itemize}
In their study only the peak presence at $\approx$ \SI{-15}{\degreeCelsius} was evaluated.

\textcite{brun.1977} proposed an approximate solution based on a \textsc{Gibbs-Thompson} relationship to convert between freezing/melting point of water and the pore radius for cylindrical pores.
The equation for freezing is:
\begin{equation}
	r_\text{P} = -\frac{64.67}{\Delta T }+0.57;~~~\SI{0}{\kelvin} > \Delta T > \SI{-40}{\kelvin},
	\label{eq:brun_freeze}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	r_\text{P} & pore radius in \si{\nano\meter}\\
	\Delta T  &  freezing point depression in \si{\kelvin},
\end{conditions}

and for melting:
\begin{equation}
	r_\text{P} = -\frac{32.33}{\Delta T }+0.68;~~~\SI{0}{\kelvin} > \Delta T > \SI{-40}{\kelvin}.
	\label{eq:brun_melt}
\end{equation}
% in Joseph.2024 stammt die + 0.68 aus "-0.12 + \delta" wobei \delta = the non-freezable layer of water in nm. With respect to the value of δ, both 1.2 nm [52,53] and 0.8 nm [51,54]

It has to be noted, that $\Delta T$ measurements below \SI{-40}{\celsius} are not covered by these equations.
However, \textcite{kloetstra.1995} proposed that it is possible to extend this lower limit.

$\Delta T$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\Delta T = T - T_\text{m}
	\label{eq:brun_dT}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	T          &   actual melting point in \si{\kelvin}, \\
	T_\text{m} &   theoretical freezing point in \si{\kelvin}.
\end{conditions}

\textcite{brun.1977} considered an adsorbed water film in the pores (thickness of \SI{.8}{\nano\meter}) and also a temperature correction for surface tension and for the melting entropy.
The relationship resulting from Equations~\ref{eq:brun_freeze} and \ref{eq:brun_melt} are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:freezing_depression}.

Furthermore, supercooling effects can be observed in the literature \cite{setzer.1994,sant.2011}, which renders the evaluation of the larger pore sizes more difficult or impossible.
They are indicated by a shift of the main freezing peak to temperatures below \SI{-10}{\degreeCelsius}.
The reason for the supercooling is the lack of nucleation points for the ice crystals.
Therefore, in more recent publications a pre-cooling step was done to induce nucleation crystals \cite{nguyen.2021,mueller.2021,joseph.2024}.

This raises the question, if the pore structure will be damaged as observable in \ac{MIP}.
\Textcite{wu.2015} concluded, that the heat flow curves were not affected by a preceding freeze cycle and there is little impact on the pore structure for porous silica.
They also applied the method to cementitious samples and detected the expectable peak shift for connected capillary pores (above \SI{-40}{\degreeCelsius}).
However, also a signal change in the isolated gel porosity (below \SI{-40}{\degreeCelsius}) was noticed.
It was assumed, that the pore connectivity changed between the measurements.
This was indicated by a signal shift in the direction of higher freezing temperatures and therefore larger pores.
%, while having limited effect on the size distribution of meso-pores.

\subsection{Other non-image-based methods to estimate the porosity}
\label{subsec:other_porosity}

Other methods to obtain information about the porosity of materials are based on their respective response on the diffusion, permeability or the sorption of various gases like \ce{N2}, \ce{O2} or water vapour.
Furthermore, the \ac{HNMR} can be used to measure the porosity of a material.

\subsubsection{\ce{^{1}H} Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (\acs{NMR})}

\ac{HNMR} can be used to evaluate the porosity of cementitious materials with no or very little Iron contents (e.g. pure phases or white cement) \cite{gran.1997,muller.2014,naber.2020}.
It is based on the magnetic resonance of the \ce{1^H} nucleus.
This allows to measure hydrated cement at room temperature without the need of drying or freezing, which would change its microstructure.
\ac{NMR} works by aligning the spin by applying a magnetic field.
If this field is turned off, which causes the aligned spins to rearrange while releasing the energy stored by aligning the spins, which is called "relaxation".

\subsubsection{Dynamic Vapour Sorption (\acs{DVS})}

\ac{DVS} can give a rough idea on the porosity of a material \cite{pinson.2015} and the behaviour of the material on changing humidity at different temperatures \cite{wang.2023}.
While the operation gas is variable, it is usually water.
Therefore, this method is useful for fully hydrated specimens.
However, for specimens younger than \SI{14}{\day} this method is not suitable, since the hydration can continue significantly during the measurement period.

The method works by placing a dry specimen in a humidity controlled chamber, while stepwise increasing the humidity inside and measuring the mass change of the specimen until mass consistency.
This is followed by a subsequent stepwise reduction of the humidity.
Depending on the available and accessible pore classes (capillary, or gel porosity), the amount of the mass change can indicate their present at different humidities during the drying step \cite{pinson.2015}.
Depending on the device used and the experiment setup, the measurements can take multiple days or weeks at room temperature and below \cite{wang.2023}.

Most authors do not directly evaluate the porosity of the specimen using this method but are using it comparatively between specimens or parameters \cite{ghantous.2023, wang.2023}.

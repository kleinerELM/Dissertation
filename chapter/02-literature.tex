\section{Cement hydration}



\section{Growth of Calcium-Silicate-Hydrate}

Why CSH-Growth is interesting? For simulation! \cite{breugel.1997,nguyen-tuan.2023,nguyen-tuan.2024}

\textsc{Anderegg} and \textsc{Hubbell} already tried 1929 / 1930 \cite{anderegg.1929,anderegg.1930} to measure the the hydration layer development of \ac{OPC} at 21$\pm$\SI{3}{\celsius} utilizing the refraction index difference between the \ac{CSH} and the unhydrated clinker.
However, they used rather coarse material.
According to their experiments, the hydrate rim is depending on the mean particle dimension (measured from 150 particles) as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel-distribution}.
This dependence becomes more prone for older specimens, since particles with a radius equal or smaller than the hydrate layer are consumed and cannot contribute to a larger hydrate layer.

In contrast to the \ac{OPC} experiments, these consisted only out of very few series of experiments with a rather high variance (see Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel}).

\textsc{Nicoleau} \cite{nicoleau.2011} measured the \ac{CSH}-thickness using Cryo-\ac{SEM} on cracked surfaces of hydrated \ac{C3S} ($w/b=0.5$, $\vartheta=$\SI{20}{\celsius}) (see Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel}).
\textsc{Bishnoi} and \textsc{Scrivener} \cite{bishnoi.2012} criticized this approach due to the rather uncontrolled cracing, the subjectivity of the measurement and the occurence of the sectioning effect, which was described in detail by \textsc{Scrivener} \cite{scrivener.2004}.
This effect is an issue for all 2D images of polished sections (e.g. typical \ac{SEM}-\ac{BSE} images) and will be described and discussed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:sectioning-effect}.

\textsc{Hader} \cite{hader.2000} and \textsc{Süß} \cite{suss.2001} manually measured the development of the length of \ac{CSH}-needles, also called \ac{CSHo}, using \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE}-images until a specimen age of \SI{28}{\day}.
\textsc{Stark} and \textsc{Möser} \cite{stark.2001, moser.2001,moser.2002} continued this work close to the 3 years mark.
The experiments were done using \ac{C3S} and $\beta$-\ac{C2S} at different temperatures.
They measured a minimum and maximum value, of which the mean was calculated.
However, it was not made clear by the authors, how many measurements were taken for each data point. Therefore, it should be assumed that these data are based on individual measurements.
The results are plotted in Figures~\ref{fig:suess_CSH_C3S} (\ac{C3S}) and \ref{fig:suess_CSH_C2S} ($\beta$-\ac{C2S}).

The formation of \ac{CSH} begins earlier with raising temperature.
This also causes a significant increase of the overall fibre length.
At the same temperature, \ac{C2S} demonstrates a slower \ac{CSH}-growth.
While the \ac{CSH}-growth of \ac{C3S} stagnates after 5 months, \textsc{Möser} indicates that the needle length of \ac{CSH} on \ac{C2S} is still increasing significantly, even until an age of 3 years.

\section{Sectioning effect}
\label{subsec:sectioning-effect}

Many measurements are done using \ac{BSE} images of embedded and polished hydrated clinker phases.
In other words, all measurements are taken on random sections through grains and their hydrate layers.
This causes an unavoidable sectioning effect \cite{scrivener.2004} as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sectioning_effect_a}.

Equations~\ref{eq:sectioning_effect_1} and \ref{eq:sectioning_effect_2} can be used to calculate the \ac{HLT} ($t$) for a spherical particle, depending on the sectioning distance from center point ($x$).

\begin{equation}
	f(x, r) = \sqrt{2r(x+r) - (x+r)^2}
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_1}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	f	&	measured \ac{HLT}, \\
	r	&   radius, \\
	x	&   sectioning distance from center point; $0 \leq x \leq r$.
\end{conditions}

\begin{equation}
	t(x) = f(x, r_\text{outer}) - f(x, r_\text{grain})
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_2}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	t				&	measured \ac{HLT}, \\
	r_\text{outer}	&   radius of the grain + hydrate layer, \\
	r_\text{grain}	&   grain radius.
\end{conditions}

\begin{equation}
	M_\text{HLT} = \frac{\int_{0}^{i} t(x) \,dx}{r_\text{grain}}
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_3}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	M_\text{HLT}	&	mean \ac{HLT} of a grain, \\
	r_\text{grain}	&   grain radius, \\
	d				&   $0 < i \leq r_\text{grain}$.
\end{conditions}

Figure~\ref{fig:sectioning_effect_b} (blue curve) indicates, that the \ac{HLT} can be significantly overestimated.
This becomes more prevalent the closer the cut through a particle is to the particle border.

\section{Correlative microscopy}

Correlative microscopy is a generic term describing the combination of multiple microscopical methods.
In the field of biology it is understood as a combination of light/fluorescence and electron microscopy \cite{mironov.2013,loussertfonta.2015}.
Nonetheless, it can be applied to most microscopic and tomographic techniques, als long as the measurement is done at exactly the same specimen with sufficient overlap \cite{loussertfonta.2015,daly.2017,mitchell.2021}.
Ideally it is done within the same device, though this is not essential.

Advantages of the combination of multiple techniques are
\begin{itemize}
    \item the combination of information of one object/phase, like:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item trace element concentrations (see Section~\ref{sec:corr_laicpms} and \cite{kleiner.2022b}),
        \item crystallographic information \cite{rossler.2022},
        \item mechanical information \cite{chen.2010,li.2025},%was von Feng - noch nicht veröffentlicht!
        \item ...
    \end{itemize}
    \item information of a specimen across multiple scales \cite{daly.2017,mitchell.2021,kleiner.2022a},
    \item The resolution differences can improve the segmentation results of lower resolution methods, enabling new insights \cite{kleiner.2022b}.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Image alignment}

Especially, if the images stem from different devices with vastly different modes, the image alignment is the most challenging part.
However, if the resulting images share enough features, they still can be aligned.
This can be done using
\begin{itemize}
    \item a fully manual alignment by rotating and transforming the images,
    \item semi-manual method by identifying matching point-pairs ($n \leq 4$) between both images \cite{},
    \item automatic methods from the field of computer vision/photogrammetry like \ac{SIFT} \cite{lowe.2004} or machine learning supported algorithms.
\end{itemize}

Usually, for few or single datasets this is done manually.
However, this can become tedious time-consuming and in many cases it is not very exact.

The main issues for combination of multi-device measurements are
\begin{enumerate}
    \item distortions,
    \item pixel aspect ratio,
    \item significant resolution differences,
    \item not enough matching features in both images,
    \item and re-identifying the measurement positions in the first place.
\end{enumerate}

To simplify the realignment, markers (called fiducial), can be used, to align different data sources to each other.
However, this is not always possible or expensive.

\section{Pore analysis}
\label{sec:pore_analysis}

On initial thought, the measurement of pores and pore sizes seems to be fairly easy.
However, while air voids in mortar and concrete are mostly spherical due to the surface tension of the fresh binder, this significantly changes below a scale of tens of micrometers, where the capillary pores begin.
These pores are filled with pore solution/water during hydration and feature much more complex, fractal-like geometries.

There are a lot of methods to measure the \ac{PSD} of a hardened specimen.
However, each method has different measurement ranges as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pore_sizes}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering

	\includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{methods/pore_sizes.pdf}
    \caption{Phase and pore sizes compared to the measurement ranges of selected methods. Redrawn and expanded according to a figure by Stark et al. \cite{stark.2000}, based on data from Romberg \cite{romberg.1978}.}
    \label{fig:pore_sizes}
\end{figure}

Image based methods like the \ac{SEM} or the \ac{CT} have already been introduced above.
However, they inherently have either resolution issues, only give a 2-dimensional view or do not give representative results due to a small \ac{ROI}.
Therefore, more indirect methods based on physical principles can help to obtain more statistically significant results.
In this thesis, \ac{MIP} and \ac{LTDSC} were used as complementary methods to the image based methods.

{\color{red} There were multiple studies trying to compare different pore analysis methods \cite{naber.2020,georget.2024}.} % more sources!

\subsection{Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (MIP)}
\label{fig:mip}

%https://www.epfl.ch/labs/lmc/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/03-Wioletta-Franco-MIP.pdf
\ac{MIP} is a rather old and well known method to measure the \ac{PSD} of porous materials \cite{romberg.1978}.
However, \ac{MIP} is also deemed inappropriate by \textsc{Diamond} to measure the \ac{PSD} using of hydrated cement, since it
\begin{iquote}
	systematically misallocates the sizes of almost all of the volume of pores \cite{diamond.2000},
\end{iquote}
which is agreed upon by others \cite{chatterji.2001,giesche.2002}.
Furthermore, it requires dried specimens, leading to a cracked and therefore non-native microstructure \cite{chatterji.2001,wang.2019}.
\textsc{Wang} et al. \cite{wang.2019} showed the damaged induced by \ac{MIP} using \ac{mCT}.
They came to the conclusion, that the pore space was increased by approximately \SI{7}{\percent} due to the mercury intrusion.
The main affected pore size range was around 100-\SI{500}{\micro\meter}.
Abell et al. \cite{abell.1999} compared \ac{MIP} with the intrusion of \textsc{Wood's} metal instead of mercury.
While the pore volume was comparable, the usage of \textsc{Wood's} metal indicated that larger pores were present.

Finally, due to the use of hazardous mercury and due to the high cost, it has a reduced relevance today.

Nevertheless, it is still a reliable method to measure open porosity down to a few \si{\nano\meter} or to compare the porosity of similar samples to each other \cite{georget.2024}.

\section{Statistical analysis of the representativeness of an 2D image}

Any statistical analysis of an image bears the question of its representativeness.
This is especially true for \ac{SEM}-analysis, since the imaged area is usually only a tiny detail of the whole specimen.
While the operator of the microscope may try to exclude unusual areas or capture a typical, representative area of the specimen, this is a highly subjective task.
To reduce this subjectiveness, one may try to obtain a larger area or set of independent images and apply statistical analysis.

In many cases, a particle size or pore size distribution is of interest.
Therefore, some authors tried to apply a \ac{REA} calculation, to make sure the observed image features are statistically relevant \cite{bosl.1998,kameda.2006,klaver.2012,houben.2014,jiang.2022}.
In the selected publications two main methods were used for this statistical analysis.

The code used to compare both methods is publicly available \cite{kleiner.2024b}. %-> https://github.com/kleinerELM/box_count_method

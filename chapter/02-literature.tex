\section{Growth of Calcium-Silicate-Hydrate}

\textsc{Anderegg} and \textsc{Hubbell} already tried 1929 / 1930 \cite{andregg.1929,andregg.1930} to measure the the hydration layer development of \ac{OPC} at 21$\pm$\SI{3}{\celsius} utilizing the refraction index difference between the \ac{CSH} and the unhydrated clinker.
However, they used rather coarse material.
According to their experiments, the hydrate rim is depending on the mean particle dimension (measured from 150 particles) as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel-distribution}.
This dependence becomes more prone for older specimens, since particles with a radius equal or smaller than the hydrate layer are consumed and cannot contribute to a larger hydrate layer.

In contrast to the \ac{OPC} experiments, these consisted only out of very few series of experiments with a rather high variance (see Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel}).

\textsc{Nicoleau} \cite{nicoleau.2011} measured the \ac{CSH}-thickness using Cryo-\ac{SEM} on cracked surfaces of hydrated \ac{C3S} ($w/b=0.5$, $\vartheta=$\SI{20}{\celsius}) (see Figure~\ref{fig:anderegg-hubbel}).
\textsc{Bishnoi} and \textsc{Scrivener} \cite{bishnoi.2012} criticized this approach due to the rather uncontrolled cracing, the subjectivity of the measurement and the occurence of the sectioning effect, which was described in detail by \textsc{Scrivener} \cite{scrivener.2004}.
This effect is an issue for all 2D images of polished sections (e.g. typical \ac{SEM}-\ac{BSE} images) and will be described and discussed in Subsection~\ref{subsec:sectioning-effect}.

\textsc{Süß} \cite{suss.2001} manually measured the development of the length of \ac{CSH}-needles, also called \ac{CSHo}, using \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE}-images until a specimen age of \SI{28}{\day}.
\textsc{Stark} and \textsc{Möser} \cite{stark.2001, moser.2001,moser.2002} continued this work close to the 3 years mark.
The experiments were done using \ac{C3S} and $\beta$-\ac{C2S} at different temperatures.
They measured a minimum and maximum value, of which the mean was calculated.
However, it was not made clear by the authors, how many measurements were taken for each data point. Therefore, it should be assumed that these data are based on individual measurements.
The results are plotted in Figures~\ref{fig:suess_CSH_C3S} (\ac{C3S}) and \ref{fig:suess_CSH_C2S} ($\beta$-\ac{C2S}).

The formation of \ac{CSH} begins earlier with raising temperature.
This also causes a significant increase of the overall fibre length.
At the same temperature, \ac{C2S} demonstrates a slower \ac{CSH}-growth. 
While the \ac{CSH}-growth of \ac{C3S} stagnates after 5 months, \textsc{Möser} indicates that the needle length of \ac{CSH} on \ac{C2S} is still increasing significantly, even until an age of 3 years.

\section{Correlative microscopy}

Correlative microscopy is a generic term describing the combination of multiple microscopical methods.
In the field of biology it is understood as a combination of light/fluorescence and electron microscopy \cite{mironov.2013,loussertfonta.2015}.
Nonetheless, it can be applied to most microscopic and tomographic techniques, als long as the measurement is done at exactly the same specimens with sufficient overlap \cite{loussertfonta.2015,daly.2017,mitchell.2021}.
Ideally it is done within the same device, though this is not essential.

Advantages of the combination of multiple techniques are
\begin{itemize}
    \item the combination of information of one object/phase, like: 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item trace element concentrations,
        \item crystallographic information,
        \item mechanical information,
        \item ...
    \end{itemize}
    \item information of a specimen across multiple scales \cite{daly.2017,mitchell.2021,kleiner.2022a},
    \item The resolution differences can improve the segmentation results of lower resolution methods, enabling new insights \cite{kleiner.2022b}.
\end{itemize}

If the resulting images share enough features to align them to each other, they still can be aligned after the measurement.
This can be done using
\begin{itemize}
    \item a fully manual alignment by rotating and transforming the image,
    \item semi-manual method by identifying matching point-pairs ($n \leq 4$) between both images,
    \item automatic methods from the field of computer vision/photogrammetry like \ac{SIFT} \cite{lowe.2004} or machine learning supported algorithms.
\end{itemize}

The alignment is not always trivial.
The main issues for combination of multi-device measurements are 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item distortions,
    \item pixel aspect ratio,
    \item significant resolution differences,
    \item not enough matching features in both images.
\end{enumerate}

For controlled specimens marks, called fiducial, can be used, to align different data sources to each other.
However, this is not always possible.

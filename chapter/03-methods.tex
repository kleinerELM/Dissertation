\chapter{Experimental Methods}

In this thesis, a myriad of different experimental and evaluation methods was used.
These methods will be detailed in this chapter.

\section{The preparation process for SEM imaging}

To obtain high-quality images using a \ac{SEM}, a lot of preparation methods are possible depending on the specimen type.
A selection of methods used within this work are listed here:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Scatter samples}. This means sprinkling powdery material over an adhesive carbon pad, followed by the removal of loose material by using compressed air. This method is fairly dangerous for the \ac{SEM} if not done properly.
	\item \textbf{Fractured surface}. Crack the specimen, lean it with compressed air and observe the freshly prepared surface.
	\item \textbf{Cross section (large scale)}. Most mineral specimens have to be embedded in epoxy resin and are then mechanically polished. (Section~\ref{sec:mech_pol}) The polishing result can be improved by \ac{ArBIB}. (Section~\ref{sec:bib_pol_moving})
	\item \textbf{Cross section (small scale)}. A small rectangular specimen (embedded or resin free) can be polished using an \ac{ArBIB} to polish an area of about \SI{1}{\square\milli\meter}. (Section~\ref{sec:bib_pol_fixed})
	\item \textbf{\Ac{FIB} cross section}. Cutting and polishing a small surface (up to 40\,$\times$\,\SI{10}{\square\micro\metre}) using \ac{FIB}. (Section~\ref{sec:fib})
	\item \textbf{Cryo preparation}. To observe water containing specimens (for slurries, pastes or very young specimens), the specimen is frozen within \SIlist{400}{\milli\second} down to about \SIlist{113}{\kelvin} at \SIlist{>\,200}{\mega\pascal} to avoid ice-crystallization. The specimen can then be processed by \ac{FIB} or by sublimation.
\end{itemize}

A selection of these methods is described in more detail in the following sections.

\subsection{Mechanical polishing}
\label{sec:mech_pol}

%The standard polished cross section is often used to obtain \ac{BSE} images.
To do a quantitative image analysis, very smooth specimen surfaces are required.
Those surfaces are usually achieved by mechanically polishing epoxy embedded specimens.

To embed the specimen, it has first to be dried at \SIrange{40}{60}{\celsius}, depending on the sensitivity of the specimen, in an oven for \SI{12}{\hour}.
The sample, which has been brought to the correct size, is now poured over with epoxy resin of a very low viscosity in a mold.
This is required to deeply penetrate the specimen and fill the majority of pores.
In this work, a combination of the resin R1370 and the hardener R1376, by Agar Scientific was used.
The embedded specimen will then be degassed in two \SI{30}{\min} cycles.

After hardening for \SI{24}{\hour} in an oven at \SI{40}{\celsius}, the specimen can be polished.
This is done in multiple steps using diamond-oil-slurries.
The particle sizes of the diamond pastes will be sequentially reduced (15, 9, 3, 1 down to \SI{0.25}{\micro\meter}).
While this polishing method is good enough for many applications like BSE- or EDX-imaging, high-resolution imaging or \ac{EBSD} require even better surface qualities.

\subsection{Argon Broad Ion Beam polishing (moving specimen)}
\label{sec:bib_pol_moving}

Most \ac{ArBIB} devices provide two modes of operation.
In one of the modes, the mechanically polished specimen is semi-erratically moved under a grazing incidence angle under the ion beam.
This method can be applied to improve or clean mechanically polished specimens or to remove thin coatings (e.g. carbon or gold) applied for previous measurements.
In this study, a triple ion beam milling system (EM TIC 3X, Leica) was used (Figure~\ref{fig:bib_device}).
The \ac{ArBIB} polishing is carried out under low pressure (\SIrange{1}{4}{\kilo\pascal}) conditions in an argon atmosphere.

\subsection{Argon Broad Ion Beam sectioning (fixed specimen)}
\label{sec:bib_pol_fixed}

Another mode requires a smaller pre-cut sample (5\,$\times$\,3\,$\times$\,\SI{10}{\cubic\milli\meter}) in a fixed sample holder.
The three argon ion beams intersect at a sharp edged tungsten mask forming a horizontal milling sector of approximately of \SI{100}{\degree} (Figure~\ref{fig:bib_device}).
The mask and the specimen surface are arranged in a way that only \SIrange{20}{100}{\micro\meter} of the sample are exposed to the ion beam above the mask (Figure~\ref{fig:bib_scheme}).

\section{SEM / Focussed Ion Beam}
\label{sec:sem}

Modern \ac{SEM}-devices can be equipped with a wide range of detectors, stages and tools.
The most common detectors are \ac{SE} and \ac{BSE} detectors of various types (e.g. \ac{ETD} or \ac{TLD}).
Most modern SEMs easily allow to image large areas using these detectors in a high resolution by acquiring multiple images and stitching them afterwards.

For chemical analysis, \ac{EDX} detectors can provide point and area mappings (see Section~\ref{sec:edx}).
A less common detector type, the \ac{EBSD}, allows to obtain crystallographic information of a clean specimen surface (see Section~\ref{sec:ebsd}).

Besides these classical detectors, modern SEMs can also be combined with a \ac{FIB}.
While this ion source allows for another way of imaging, it also enables to cut the specimen or deposit coatings (e.g. carbon or platinum) at specified locations (see Section~\ref{sec:fib}).

While the \ac{SEM} is widley used to analyze cement based materials, it has its pitfalls.
In contrast to an light microscope, it can alter the specimen during the observation significantly.
For example, early publications define \ac{CSH} as gel.
In one part, this due to its low crystallinity, which is not detectable using \ac{XRD} but only \ac{TEM}.
On the other hand, the \ac{CSH}-crystals appeared gel like due to preparation artifacts and severe electron beam damage of the water containing phases.
This causes them to melt into a gel-like structure, hence its early name.
Nowadays, this is less of an issue anymore due to significant progress in the sensitivity of the detectors of electron microscopes.
However, to obtain enough signal for some detectors (e.g. \ac{EDX} or \ac{EBSD}), intensive electron beam exposure might still be required, which can cause visible damage \cite{rossen.2017}.
Also, at very high magnifications, the energy density is very high even at low voltages and currents.
This can cause \ac{CSH}-structures to bent or melt and introduce pores within originally compact phases (Figure~\ref{fig:ebeam_damage}).

\subsection{Sectioning effect}
\label{subsec:sectioning-effect}

Many measurements are done using \ac{BSE} images of embedded and polished hydrated clinker phases.
In other words, all measurements are taken on random sections through grains and their hydrate layers.
This causes an unavoidable sectioning effect \cite{scrivener.2004} as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sectioning_effect_a}.

Equations~\ref{eq:sectioning_effect_1} and \ref{eq:sectioning_effect_2} can be used to calculate the \ac{HLT} ($t$) for a spherical particle, depending on the sectioning distance from center point ($x$).

\begin{equation}
	f(x, r) = \sqrt{2r(x+r) - (x+r)^2}
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_1}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	f	&	measured \ac{HLT}, \\
	r	&   radius, \\
	x	&   sectioning distance from center point; $0 \leq x \leq r$.
\end{conditions}

\begin{equation}
	t(x) = f(x, r_\text{outer}) - f(x, r_\text{grain})
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_2}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	t				&	measured \ac{HLT}, \\
	r_\text{outer}	&   radius of the grain + hydrate layer, \\
	r_\text{grain}	&   grain radius.
\end{conditions}

\begin{equation}
	M_\text{HLT} = \frac{\int_{0}^{i} t(x) \,dx}{r_\text{grain}}
	\label{eq:sectioning_effect_3}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	M_\text{HLT}	&	mean \ac{HLT} of a grain, \\
	r_\text{grain}	&   grain radius, \\
	d				&   $0 < i \leq r_\text{grain}$.
\end{conditions}

Figure~\ref{fig:sectioning_effect_b} (blue curve) indicates, that the \ac{HLT} can be significantly overestimated.
This becomes more prevalent the closer the cut through a particle is to the particle border.
% https://www.wolframalpha.com/input?i=integral&assumption=%7B%22F%22%2C+%22Integral%22%2C+%22rangestart%22%7D+-%3E%220%22&assumption=%7B%22C%22%2C+%22integral%22%7D+-%3E+%7B%22Calculator%22%2C+%22dflt%22%7D&assumption=%7B%22F%22%2C+%22Integral%22%2C+%22integrand%22%7D+-%3E%22sqrt%282*1.3*%28x%2B1.3%29+-+%28x%2B1.3%29%5E2%29+-+sqrt%282*1*%28x%2B1%29+-+%28x%2B1%29%5E2%29%22&assumption=%7B%22F%22%2C+%22Integral%22%2C+%22rangeend%22%7D+-%3E%22.9%22

\subsection{Energy-Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)}
\label{sec:edx}

\ac{EDX} is a widely used technology available even on smaller \ac{SEM}-devices.
It is based on the interaction of the electron beam with the sample surface.
The electrons in the atoms of the sample are excited by the electron beam and subsequently emit an individual X-ray pattern that enables the source atoms to be identified.
However, the excitation voltage has to be about twice as high as the X-ray emission lines of the expected elements to collect enough counts to identify and quantify it.% \acs{Ka}  or \acs{Kb}
This results in a minimal required electron beam voltage of \SI{12}{\kilo\volt} to measure Fe with a \ac{Ka} of \SI{6.4}{\kilo\electronvolt} \cite{hudson.2003}.

However, a high powered electron beam can also damage specimens.
For example, \ac{CSH} phases and portlandite may be dehydrated after a prolonged \ac{EDX}-measurement.
This may result in a geometrical deformation as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ebeam_damage} or may also introduce pores with a diameter of several \SI{}{\nano\meter} as demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:edx_damage_a} and \ref{fig:edx_damage_b} which are slices of a \ac{FIB}-stack before and after an \ac{EDX}-measurement.

\subsection{Serial sectioning using Focussed Ion Beam (FIB)}
\label{sec:fib}

The \ac{FIB} allows to manipulate specimens.
Instead of electrons, Ga-ions are accelerated to the specimen surface.
This allows the targeted removal of material or to deposit coatings to protect a region of interest, to glue  the specimen to a surface or to make a surface conductive.
The device used in this thesis provides carbon an platinum coatings.

These capabilities, allow to capture three-dimensional datasets using one or a combination of the detectors available (e.g. \ac{BSE}, \ac{EDX}, \ac{EBSD}).
In contrast to X-ray computer tomography, this method is intrinsic destructive.

The cementitious specimen should be embedded in resin and at least mechanically polished to find a suitable \ac{ROI} using the \ac{BSE} detector.
Resin filled pores have advantages for later pore segmentation.

The ion and the electron beam are arranged in an angle of \SI{52}{\degree}, while the ion beam hits the specimen in an angle of \SI{90}{\degree} as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fib_scheme}.
The \ac{ROI} has to be in the eucentric point (point of rotation/intersection point of the electron and ion beam).
This means, if the specimen is tilted, the point does not move in the life image and therefore is in the rotational center.

The standard method to obtain \ac{FIBnt}-images is to dig trenches around the \ac{ROI} (typically 10\,$\times$\,10\,$\times$\,\SI{10}{\cubic\micro\meter}) as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fib_top}.
Beforehand, the \ac{ROI} is coated with a layer of platinum for protection and a fiducial side is set.
The fiducial is required to correct for specimen drift during the cutting process.
After a polishing run and the imaging setup, the cutting process can start.
The ion cutting is done at \SI{30}{\kilo\volt} and \SI{0.11}{\nano\ampere}. % check ampere setting for cutting
Each slice can be cut down to a thickness of \SI{10}{\nano\meter}.
After cutting, the slice can be imaged using multiple detectors.

Depending on the detector type and the geometrical positioning of the detector there might be some imaging limits and the imaging should not be done for every slice.
For example, the \ac{EDX}-detector requires high voltages for a useful signal, which might damage the specimen, or the specimen has to be rotated to obtain a \ac{EBSD} signal.
Furthermore, both detectors require a lot of measurement time, which significantly influences the overall measuring duration.
The recording of a 10\,$\times$\,10\,$\times$\,\SI{10}{\cubic\micro\meter} cube using only a high-resolution BSE detector takes about {\color{red} \SI{24}{\hour}} excluding the preparation.

The cutting process can introduce some typical artifacts like curtaining or vertical shadowing.
Curtaining is caused by the cutting process causing vertical line artifacts in the direction of the ion beam (Figure~\ref{fig:fib_artifacts_a}).
These artifacts may be reduced in post processing using a directional \ac{FFT} based filter \cite{fitschen.2017,schankula.2018}.
The shadowing effect is caused by a loss of signal of the back-scattered electrons due to the trench geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:fib_artifacts_b}).
This well known effect can be fairly easily corrected in post processing {\color{red} \cite{fager.2020}}. %are there more publications?

However, there are more severe uncorrectable artifacts.
Since cementitious material is not conductive and the trenches are not coated throughout, charging effects can negatively influence the image quality (e.g. Figure~\ref{fig:fib_artifacts_c} and \ref{fig:fib_artifacts_a}) or cause uncorrectable image drift during the process.
The re-deposition of the removed material usually happens within the trenches, which does not cause any problems.
However, it may also appear on small structures in front of the \ac{ROI} or even on the specimen surface after longer imaging periods as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fib_artifacts_b} and \ref{fig:fib_artifacts_c}.

To avoid or at least reduce these issues, the \ac{ROI} can be completely cut free and a lift-out process, similar to the preparation process of \ac{TEM}-samples {\color{red} \cite{giannuzzi.2005}}, can be done. %there has to be a source from 1997 or earlier describing the lift-out process...
To do this, a needle is inserted and welded to the \ac{ROI}-cube using the platinum coating process.
Afterwards, the cube is complete cut loose from the bulk material, and a copper comb is inserted to the SEM.
Finally, the cube is transferred and then welded to a finger of the comb as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fib_liftout}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering

	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{methods/fib_liftout.jpg}
    \caption{\SI{20}{\micro\meter} wide specimen (A). On the left is the needle welded to the specimen (B). They are navigated and welded to the copper comb (C). The platinum vapor source (D) used for welding is visible on the right. \cite{kleiner.2021}}
    \label{fig:fib_liftout}
\end{figure}

The so prepared cube can now be serial cut as normally with significantly reduced shadowing and charging effects.

\section{Laser Ablation - Inductively Coupled Plasma - Mass Spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS)}

A \ac{LAICPMS} consists of a laser to ablate the specimen surface, a microscope to aim the laser at the measurement side, a \ac{ICP} to ionize the ablated flow of material within a carrier gas and a \ac{MS}.
This allows to detect minor and trace elements as well as isotopes in a spatially resolved manner.
Therefore, \ac{LAICPMS} is in wide use as an analytical method to determine the age of geological samples \cite{liu.2013} or for spatially resolved elemental mapping of heavy metals in biological tissue \cite{limbeck.2015}.

The laser ablation was done with a ns-Nd:YAG-laser at the quintupled wavelength of 213\,nm (\textit{ESI NWR 213}).
A quadrupole \ac{ICP}-\ac{MS} (\textit{NexION 300D} from \textit{Perkin Elmer}) was used for the elemental analysis.
The carrier gas chosen was helium due to its well-known advantages over other transport gases, such as its higher sensitivity for most \ac{ICP}-\ac{MS} instruments and smaller particle size distributions \cite{gunther.1999}.
The samples were placed in a two-volume sample cell and purged for at least \SI{10}{\min}.

\ac{LAICPMS} intrinsically results in a damaged surface, since it vaporizes material.
Figure~\ref{fig:laicpms-pre-ablation} illustrates this effect on a clinker surface.
It also indicates the the spot size (6 $\times$ \SI{6}{\micro\meter}) and the rough interaction depth, which is approximately \SI{7}{\micro\meter}.
On the positive side, this damage can be use for cross-method alignment purposes.

\section{Pore size measurement}

On initial thought, the measurement of pores seems to be fairly easy.
However, while air voids in mortar and concrete are mostly spherical due to the surface tension of the fresh binder, this significantly changes below a scale of tens of micrometers, where the capillary pores begin.
These pores feature much more complex, fractal like geometries.

There are a lot of methods to measure the \ac{PSD} of a hardened specimen.
However, each method has different measurement ranges as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pore_sizes}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering

	\includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{methods/pore_sizes.pdf}
    \caption{Phase and pore sizes compared to the measurement ranges of selected methods. Redrawn and expanded according to a figure by Stark et al. \cite{stark.2000}, based on data from Romberg \cite{romberg.1978}.} % nach Stark auch Setzer?
    \label{fig:pore_sizes}
\end{figure}
%https://www.epfl.ch/labs/lmc/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/03-Wioletta-Franco-MIP.pdf

Image based methods like the \ac{SEM} or the \ac{mCT} have already been introduced above.
However, they inherently have either resolution issues, only give a 2-dimensional view or do not give representative results due to a small \ac{ROI}.
Therefore, more indirect methods based on physical principles can help to obtain more statistically significant results.
In this thesis, \ac{MIP} and \ac{LTDSC} were used as complementary methods to the image based methods.

\subsection{Mercury Intrusion Porosimetry (MIP)}
\label{fig:mip}

\ac{MIP} is a rather old and well known method to measure the \ac{PSD} of porous materials \cite{romberg.1978}.
However, \ac{MIP} is also deemed inappropriate by Diamond \cite{diamond.2000} to measure the \ac{PSD} using of hydrated cement, since it \enquote{systematically misallocates the sizes of almost all of the volume of pores}, which is agreed upon by others \cite{chatterji.2001,giesche.2002} .
Furthermore, due to the use of hazardous mercury and therefore high cost, it has a reduced relevance today.
Nevertheless, it is a reliable method to measure open porosity down to a few \SI{}{\nano\meter}.
In this thesis, an \textit{Autopore IV 9500} from \textit{Micromeritics GmbH} was used for the \ac{MIP} investigations.
It requires small pieces of dried specimen of about 5\,$\times$\,5\,$\times$\,\SI{5}{\cubic\micro\meter}.
It stepwise covers an pressure range of 0.003 to \SI{206}{\mega\pascal}, which roughly translates to 0.002 to \SI{200}{\micro\meter}.
However, values above {\color{red} ~\SI{20}{\micro\meter} should be ignored, since these are mostly cracks}.% geschätzt aus bettinas arbeit

\subsection{Low Temperature - Differential Scanning Calorimetry (LT-DSC)}
\label{fig:ltdsc}

Another less common method to indirectly measure the \ac{PSD}, especially in the nano-pore range, is the \ac{LTDSC}.
Its principle is based on the freezing behavior of pore water, the pore type and pore size.
Brun et al. \cite{brun.1977} proposed an approximate solution to convert between freezing/melting point of water and the pore radius for cylindrical pores.
The equation for freezing is :
\begin{equation}
	r_\text{P} = -\frac{64.67}{\Delta T }+0.57;~~~\SI{0}{\kelvin} > \Delta T > \SI{-40}{\kelvin},
	\label{eq:brun_freeze}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	r_\text{P} & pore radius in \si{\nano\meter}\\
	\Delta T  &  freezing point depression in \si{\kelvin},
\end{conditions}

and for melting:
\begin{equation}
	r_\text{P} = -\frac{32.33}{\Delta T }+0.68;~~~\SI{0}{\kelvin} > \Delta T > \SI{-40}{\kelvin},
	\label{eq:brun_melt}
\end{equation}

while $\Delta T$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\Delta T = T - T_\text{m}
	\label{eq:brun_dT}
\end{equation}
\begin{conditions}
	T          &   actual melting point in \si{\kelvin}, \\
	T_\text{m} &   theoretical freezing point in \si{\kelvin}.
\end{conditions}

However, $\Delta T$ measurements below \SI{-40}{\celsius} are not covered by these equations.

Brun et al. \cite{brun.1977} considered an adsorbed water film in the pores (thickness of \SI{.8}{\nano\meter}) and also a temperature correction for surface tension and for the melting entropy.
The relationship resulting from Equations~\ref{eq:brun_freeze} and \ref{eq:brun_melt} are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:freezing_depression}.

However, this also depends on the pore type.
For example, water in connected capillary pores freezes until \SI{-20}{\celsius}, isolated pores between \SIrange{-20}{-35}{\celsius} and gel pores freeze below \SI{-35}{\celsius}.
This is also illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:freeze}.

\section{\acs*{SEM}}
\label{sec:sem}

For most of the \ac{SEM}-imaging and for all \ac{FIB} experiments, a \textit{thermoFischer Scientific Helios G4 UX} was used.
It is equipped with additional detectors by \textit{Oxford Instruments}: two combinable \ac{EDX} detectors (\textit{XMax$^\text{N}$ 80} with a polymer window and the windowless \textit{Ultim Extreme}) and the \ac{EBSD} detector \textit{C-Swift}.
For cryo-preparation, the microscope is equipped with a cooling stage and vacuum cryo transfer system \textit{EM VCT500} from \textsc{Leica}.

Some of the \ac{SEM}-imaging was done utilizing a \textit{Nova NanoSEM 230} by \textit{FEI}.


\section{The preparation process for \acs*{SEM} imaging}

To obtain high-quality images using a \ac{SEM}, a lot of preparation methods are possible depending on the specimen type.
In most cases, except for cryo-preparation, the specimen has first to be immersed in isopropanol to stop the hydration process.
Subsequently, the specimen has to be dried below \qty{60}{\celsius} to avoid damage to the water containing phases.

A selection of the preparation methods used within this work are listed here:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Scatter sample}: Sprinkling powdery material over an adhesive carbon pad, followed by the removal of loose material by using compressed air.
	This preparation method can fairly dangerous for the \ac{SEM} if not done properly.
	Loose particles can damage detectors or the high-vacuum pumps during venting or due to charging.
	\item \textbf{Fractured surface}: Crack the specimen, lean it with compressed air and observe the freshly prepared surface.
	\item \textbf{Cross section (large scale)}: Most mineral specimens have to be embedded in epoxy resin before they are mechanically polished. (\zcref{sec:mech_pol}) The polishing result can then be improved by applying \ac{ArBIB} polishing. (\zcref{sec:bib_pol_moving})
	\item \textbf{Cross section (small scale)}: A small rectangular specimen (embedded or resin free) can be polished using an \ac{ArBIB} to polish an area of about \qty{1}{\square\milli\meter}. (\zcref{sec:bib_pol_fixed})
	\item \textbf{\Ac{FIB} cross section}: Cutting and polishing a small surface (up to \qtyproduct{40 x 10}{\square\micro\metre}) using \ac{FIB}. (\zcref{sec:fib})
	\item \textbf{Cryo-preparation}: To observe water containing specimens (slurries, pastes or very young specimens), the specimen is frozen within \qty{400}{\milli\second} down to about \qty{113}{\kelvin} at \qty{>\,200}{\mega\pascal} to avoid ice-crystal formation. (\zcref{sec:cryofibnt}).
\end{itemize}

A selection of these methods is described in more detail in the following sections.

\subsection{Mechanical polishing}
\label{sec:mech_pol}

%The standard polished cross section is often used to obtain \ac{BSE} images.
To do a quantitative image analysis, very smooth specimen surfaces are required.
Those surfaces are usually achieved by mechanically polishing epoxy resin embedded specimens.

Before embedding the specimen, the hydration process of young specimens has to be stopped first by immersing them into isopropanol to remove the water.
Nevertheless, building materials have to be dried before the embedding process.
Depending on the sensitivity of the specimen, they will remain at \qtyrange{40}{60}{\celsius} in an oven for \qty{12}{\hour}.
The sample, which has been brought to the correct size, is now poured over with epoxy resin of a very low viscosity in a mold.
This is required to deeply penetrate the specimen and fill the majority of pores.
In this work, a combination of the resin \textit{R1370} and the hardener \textit{R1376}, by Agar Scientific was used.
The embedded specimen will then be degassed in two \qty{30}{\min} cycles.

After hardening for \qty{24}{\hour} in an oven at \qty{40}{\celsius}, the specimen can be polished.
This is done in multiple steps using diamond-oil-slurries.
The particle sizes of the diamond pastes will be sequentially reduced (\num{15}, \num{9}, \num{3}, \num{1} and finally \qty{0.25}{\micro\meter}).

\subsection{\Acl*{ArBIB} preparation}

\ac{ArBIB} is a technique which enables the polishing or milling of a specimen to obtain very clean and flat surfaces utilising one or more \ce{Ar}-ion beams.
In this study, a triple ion beam milling system (\textit{EM TIC 3X}, \textsc{Leica}) was used (\zcref{fig:bib_device}).
The \ac{ArBIB} polishing is carried out under low pressure (\qtyrange{1}{4}{\kilo\pascal}) conditions in an argon atmosphere.
The \ac{ArBIB} device used provides two modes of operation: \ac{ArBIB} polishing or \ac{ArBIB} sectioning.

\subsection{\acs*{ArBIB} polishing}
\label{sec:bib_pol_moving}

In the polishing mode, the mechanically polished specimen is semi-randomly moved under a grazing incidence angle under the Argon ion beam.
This method can be applied to improve or clean mechanically polished specimens or to remove thin coatings (e.g. carbon or gold) applied for previous measurements.
This technique is mostly used to improve surfaces for \ac{EDX}- or \ac{EBSD}-measurements, where very clean surfaces are required \cite{rossler.2017,rossler.2022}

\subsection{\acs*{ArBIB} sectioning}
\label{sec:bib_pol_fixed}

The sectioning mode requires a smaller pre-cut sample (\qtyproduct{5 x 3 x 10}{\cubic\milli\meter}) in a fixed sample holder.
The three argon ion beams intersect at a sharp edged tungsten mask forming a horizontal milling sector of $\approx$ \qty{100}{\degree} (\zcref{fig:bib_device}).
The mask and the specimen surface are arranged in a way that only \qtyrange{20}{100}{\micro\meter} of the sample are exposed to the ion beam above the mask (\zcref{fig:bib_scheme}).

This process causes some artifacts, which affect the evaluation of the resulting images.
However, the resulting surface quality still exceeds any mechanical preparation.

As indicated in \zcref{fig:bib_scheme} as `irradiation area', the ion beam can induce heat in the surface of the specimen during milling and polishing.
Therefore, the ion beam device used, allows to cool the specimen below \qty{-140}{\celsius} using liquid nitrogen.
This helps to avoid that the specimen is heated above damaging temperature (\qty{60}{\celsius} for \ac{CSH} \cite{he.2018}).

Due to variations in hardness among the phases within a material, the ion beam can also induce an artefact called the `curtaining effect' \cite{desbois.2013}, which is also known in the context of \ac{SEM}-\ac{FIB} \cite{cantoni.2014}.
These stripes align with the direction of the ion beams, as harder phases or pores create `shadows' of the material in their wake.
While these can be reduced in the \ac{SEM}-\ac{FIB} process using coatings \cite{cantoni.2014}, this is not possible in the \ac{ArBIB} process.
These artefacts can be significantly reduced in a subsequent image processing step by applying a \ac{FFT} \cite{kleiner.2021a}, as demonstrated for images from a \ac{SEM}-\ac{FIB} process \cite{fitschen.2017,schankula.2018}.
This removes the stripes in one direction, however, this filter can also soften the image.
This is especially apparent when artefacts are present in more than one direction in the image \cite{kleiner.2021a}.

\section{Tomography utilizing ion beams and laser milling}

Since the 2000s, multiple slice-based tomography methods were developed to obtain microscopical 3D volumes of a sample.
\textcite{desbois.2013} demonstrated that \ac{ArBIB} can be used for tomography by stepwise milling large areas (up to a few \unit{\square\micro\meter}) of a mineralogical sample in a \ac{SEM} device.
However, the surface roughness was still rather high (\qty{\pm 40}{\nano\meter}), reducing the effective resolution in the $z$ direction significantly.

A very common method is the \ac{FIBnt}, which was also used in this study.

\subsection{\Acl*{FIBnt} (\acs*{FIBnt})}
\label{sec:fib}

To apply this method, the cementitious specimen was embedded in resin to avoid imaging the pore background or loose moving material within larger pores.
Resin filled pores have advantages for later pore segmentation.
The specimen should be mechanically polished to find a suitable \ac{ROI} using the \ac{BSE} detector.

The established method to obtain \ac{FIBnt}-images is to dig trenches around the \ac{ROI} (typically a square with an edge length of \qtyrange{10}{20}{\micro\meter}) as illustrated in \zcref{fig:fib_top}.
Beforehand, the \ac{ROI} is coated with a layer of platinum for protection and a fiducial side is set.
The fiducial is required to correct for specimen drift during the cutting process.
After a polishing run and the imaging setup, the cutting process can start (see \zcref{fig:fib_scheme}).
For small volumes, the slicing is done at \qty{30}{\kilo\volt} and \qty{0.11}{\nano\ampere}.
However, for large volumes, the preparation time can  % TODO: check ampere setting for cutting
Each slice can be cut down to a thickness of \qty{10}{\nano\meter}.
After cutting, the slice can be imaged using multiple detectors.

Depending on the detector type and the geometrical positioning of the detectors there might be some imaging limitations.
Often it is not necessary or advisable to use detectors like \ac{EDX} or \ac{EBSD} for every slice.
The \ac{EDX}-detector requires higher accelerating voltages for a useful signal, which might damage the specimen.
To obtain \ac{EBSD} data, the specimen has to be rotated to face the detector, which causes a significant increase of the measurement time.
The recording of a \qtyproduct{10 x 10 x 10}{\cubic\micro\meter} cube using only a high-resolution BSE detector takes about \qtyrange{12}{24}{\hour} excluding the sample preparation.
Combined with \ac{EDX} imaging, the time can increase to multiple days for a single cube.

\subsection{Laser preparation and tomography}

In recent years, a new high-throughput technique has emerged using a \ac{fs-laser} to prepare specimen volumes on the order of several hundred~\unit{\micro\meter}.
In this work, the \ac{fs-laser} preparation device \textsc{microPREP PRO FEMTO} by \textsc{3D Micromac} was used to prepare selected areas for a subsequent \ac{FIBnt} process.
The device is equipped with a \textsc{Carbide CB5} \ac{fs-laser} source (pulse duration ca.~\qty{211}{\femto\second}, wavelength of \qty{515}{\nano\meter}, up to \qty{6}{\watt} average power and \qty{100}{\micro\joule} maximum pulse energy) by \textsc{Light Conversion}.
A prism with a width of \qtyrange{40}{50}{\micro\meter} and a length of \qty{750}{\micro\meter} was prepared from the bulk material.
At the sides, trenches of \qty{280}{\micro\meter} and in the front a trench of \qty{250}{\micro\meter} were removed.
The \ac{fs-laser} was set to a low power setting at \qty{500}{\milli\watt}, while omitting three out of four pulses, to reduce the accumulation of heat within the material.
The process for one site took about \qty{90}{\second}.

\section{Cryo-preparation for \acs*{SEM} and \acs*{FIB}}
\label{sec:cryofibnt}

Cryo-preparation for \ac{SEM} and \ac{FIB}, respectively, is a method to obtain close-to-nature high-resolution images of water containing specimens (e.g. slurries, pastes or very young specimens \cite{holzer.2007, zingg.2008a}).
In this process, the specimen is frozen within a very short time down to about \qty{113}{\kelvin} at >\,\qty{0.2}{\giga\pascal} to avoid ice-crystal formation.

A small amount of the liquid specimen is placed within a sandwich of two circular brass sample holders (diameter: \qty{3}{\milli\meter}, depth: \qty{0.5}{\milli\meter}; \textit{type `B'}, \textsc{BalTec}; see \zcref{fig:cryo-prep}).
The sample is then frozen under high pressure (using an \textit{EM HPM100} by \textsc{Leica}) and transferred to a high vacuum preparation system (\textit{EM MED020} by \textsc{Leica}).
The sample holder is then opened under cooled conditions using a knife.
Then, the specimen is transferred in vacuum and under cooled conditions to the cryo-stage within the \ac{SEM}.
To avoid charging effects, the specimen can be coated with a layer of \ce{Au} in the high vacuum preparation system after splitting open the cooled sample holder.

\section[Laser Ablation - Inductively Coupled Plasma - Mass Spectrometry \\ (\acs*{LAICPMS})]{Laser Ablation - Inductively Coupled Plasma - Mass Spectrometry (\acs*{LAICPMS})}
% The text for the TOC contains a line break, since the line was overflowing - it is not a nice fix but it kind of works...

A \ac{LAICPMS} consists of a laser to ablate the specimen surface, a microscope to aim the laser at the measurement side, an \ac{ICP} to ionize the ablated flow of material within a carrier gas, and a \ac{MS}.
This allows to detect minor and trace elements as well as isotopes in a spatially resolved manner.
Therefore, \ac{LAICPMS} is in wide use as an analytical method to determine the age of geological samples \cite{liu.2013} and for spatially resolved elemental mapping of heavy metals in biological tissue \cite{limbeck.2015}.

The laser ablation was performed by a \unit{\nano\second}-\ce{Nd}:YAG-laser at the quintupled wavelength of \qty{213}{\nano\meter} (\textit{ESI NWR 213} from \textsc{Elemental Scientific Inc.}).
A quadrupole \ac{ICP}-\ac{MS} (\textit{NexION 300D} from \textsc{Perkin Elmer}) was used for the elemental analysis.
The carrier gas was helium due to its well-known advantages over other transport gases, such as its higher sensitivity for most \ac{ICP}-\ac{MS} instruments and smaller resulting particle size distributions \cite{gunther.1999}.
The samples were placed in a two-volume sample cell and purged for at least \qty{10}{\min}.

\ac{LAICPMS} intrinsically results in a damaged surface, since it vaporizes material.
\zcref{fig:laicpms-pre-ablation} illustrates this effect on a clinker surface.
It also indicates the the spot size (\qtyproduct{6 x 6}{\micro\meter\squared}) and the rough interaction depth, which is $\approx$ \qty{7}{\micro\meter}.
Conveniently, this damage can be use for cross-method alignment purposes.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{\acs*{MIP}}
\label{sec:method-mip}

For this work, an \textit{Autopore IV 9500} from \textit{Micromeritics GmbH} was used for the \ac{MIP} measurements.
It requires small pieces of dried specimen of about \qtyproduct{5 x 5 x 5}{\cubic\micro\meter}.
It stepwise covers an pressure range of \qtyrange{0.003}{206}{\mega\pascal}.
This pressure range can be used to measure a range of pore sizes of \qtyrange{0.002}{200}{\micro\meter}.
However, larger values (above $\approx$ \qty{10}{\micro\meter}) will be ignored, since these can be attributed to air voids and (micro-)cracks. % geschätzt aus bettinas arbeit

The hardened specimens were sliced to fit the small aluminum crucibles with a inner volume of $\approx$ \qty{60}{\micro\litre}.
These crucibles sealed air tight by cold-welding a lid under pressure and measured against an empty reference crucible.

The temperature regime used is shown in \zcref{fig:ltdc_regime}.
The indicated zones {\color{blue}\ding{100}1} and {\color{blue}\ding{100}2}, respectively, are intended to create ice seeds and thus, avoid supercooling.
Data from the measurement passes P1 (down to \qty{-25}{\celsius}) and P2 (down to \qty{-60}{\celsius}) provide the data that can be evaluated for the porosity analysis.
A cooling and heating rate of $\pm$\,\qty{2}{\kelvin\per\min} was applied, since it is the fastest usable heating rate according to \textcite{neffati.2001}.
The difference between those two measurement zones allows to distinguish the ice mass of pores with radii smaller (P2) and larger (P1) than $\approx$ \qty{3}{\nano\meter}.

During supercooling the enthalpy of the melting of ice ($H_\text{fus}(T)$ in \unit{\joule\per\gram}) is changing slightly.
Therefore, at first this enthalpy has to be approximated for every $T$ using \zcref{eq:ltdsc_h} by \textcite{chen.1988} and \textcite{ishikiriyama.1995} based on data from \textcite{randall.1930}:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\Delta H_\text{fus}(T) = \qty{334.1}{\joule\per\gram} & + \qty{2.119}{\joule\per\gram\per\kelvin} \cdot ( \Delta T_\text{fus} ) \\
		& - \qty{0.000783}{\joule\per\gram\per\kelvin\squared} \cdot ( \Delta T_\text{fus} )^2 ,
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:ltdsc_h}
\end{equation}

where $\Delta T_\text{fus}$ is the temperature difference between the freezing point $T_{\text{fus},0}$ and the current temperature $T$.

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\Delta T_\text{fus} = T - T_{\text{fus},0} = T - \qty{273.15}{\kelvin}
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Then the measured heat flow signal $\dot{Q}_{raw}(T)$ caused by the phase transition has to be prepared by subtracting a baseline $\dot{Q}_\text{B}(T)$.
The baseline represents the heat capacity of the solid matrix and the unfrozen liquid/ice fractions.
A linear approximation of the baseline is established by fitting the steady-state heat flow signal in temperature ranges free from phase transition peaks, which have to be found manually.

\begin{equation}
	\dot{Q}_{m}(T) = \dot{Q}_\text{raw}(T) -\dot{Q}_\text{B}(T)
	\label{eq:mass_heat_flow}
\end{equation}


After the baseline correction, the total ice mass can be calculated by integrating the heat flow curve over the melting peak.
%\dot{Q}/m_\text{specimen} = DSC / Wärmestrom pro Probenmasse
% \begin{equation}
% 	m_\text{ice} = \int_{\vartheta_\text{min}}^{\vartheta_\text{max}} \dot{Q}_{m}(\vartheta) d\vartheta  / \Delta H_\text{fus}
% 	\label{eq:ltdsc_ice}
% \end{equation}

The heat flow per unit mass $\dot{Q}_{m}$ is related to the rate of ice mass formation $\dot{m}_\text{ice}$ and the enthalpy of fusion $H_\text{fus}$.
Since $\dot{Q}_{m}=\dot{m}_\text{ice}\cdot H_\text{fus}$, the total mass is found by integrating the mass rate over the time interval of the measurement:

\begin{equation}
	m_\text{ice} = \int_{t_{\text{min}}}^{t_{\text{max}}} \frac{\dot{Q}_{m}(T)}{\Delta H_\text{fus}(T)} dt
	\label{eq:ltdsc_ice_integral}
\end{equation}

The ice mass of the gel pores $m_\text{gel}$ can then be estimated by subtracting the ice mass of the capillary pores ($m_\text{P1}$, P1 zone) from the total measured ice mass ($m_\text{P2}$, P2 zone):

\begin{equation}
	m_\text{gel} = m_\text{P2} - m_\text{P1}
	\label{eq:ltdsc_ice2}
\end{equation}


The ice density $\rho_\text{ice}$ is required for converting the calculated ice mass to a volume.
However, the ice density significatly depends on its temperature.
The density can be calculated using \zcref{eq:ice_density} \cite{mueller.2021} which was fitted using data published in \cite{eisenberg.2005}:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\rho_\text{ice} = \qty{0.9167}{\gram\per\centi\meter\cubed} & - \qty{2.053e-4}{\gram\per\centi\meter\cubed\per\kelvin} \cdot ( \Delta T_\text{fus} ) \\
		& - \qty{1.357e-6}{\gram\per\centi\meter\cubed\per\kelvin\squared} \cdot ( \Delta T_\text{fus} )^2
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:ice_density}
\end{equation}

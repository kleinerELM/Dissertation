\chapter[Argon broad ion beam preparation techniques]{\Acl*{ArBIB} preparation techniques}
\label{sec:arbib-chapter}

\ac{ArBIB} preparation is often used for the surface cleaning of specimens for \ac{EBSD} measurements.
However, it is mostly known and used for materials other than cement \cite{desbois.2017}. % and clay \cite{desbois.2017}.
For the preparation of cementitious specimens it is still rarely applied.

\section{\acs*{ArBIB} polishing}
\label{sec:bib_pol_moving_result}

While the ion polishing is mostly used to enable \ac{EBSD}-measurements, it can also allow artefact free imaging of cementitious materials using the \ac{SE} and \ac{BSE} mode at low voltages (e.g., \qty{2}{\kilo\volt}).
This is demonstrated in \zcref{fig:BSE_CEMI_voltages} using a three year old hydrated CEM~I sample, which was first carbon coated and then cleaned using \ac{ArBIB} (\qty{6}{\kilo\volt} at \qty{2}{\milli\ampere} for \qty{20}{\minute}).
There is a significant difference in phase contrast, detail and susceptibility to charging artefacts at different voltages and with and without a carbon coating.
All images were recorded at \qty{16}{\bit} color depth.

\begin{figure*}[htb]
    \centering

	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{methods/bse-voltages/marked.pdf}
    \caption{
    	\ac{SEM} images of CEM~I hydrated for three years. With a carbon coating (top row) and after a subsequent \ac{ArBIB} polishing (middle row).Recorded using different voltages and at \qty{0.8}{\nano\ampere}.
		On the bottom, a comparison of the phase contrast between \ac{CH} and \ac{CSH} at \num{2.0} (\ac{ArBIB} polished, {\color{cyan}blue}) and \qty{1}{\kilo\volt} (\ce{C} coated, {\color{red}red}) is illustrated.
    }
    \label{fig:BSE_CEMI_voltages}
\end{figure*}

There is no fixed grey value for a phase in \ac{BSE} imaging.
It can be manipulated in device not only by changing the voltage and current, but also by changing brightness, gamma and contrast values either automatically or manually.
Therefore, the contrast of the \qty{16}{bit} images was normalised using the \textit{Enhance Contrast} function available in \textsc{ImageJ}.
In some instances this significantly changed the appearance of the images.

The \ac{BSE} image of a carbon coated specimen mostly shows the coating surface at \qty{2.5}{\kilo\volt} and the chemical contrast is only slightly visible.
At \qty{4.0}{\kilo\volt} artefacts are still very prominent, making the image unusable for any segmentation task.
An artefact free image can be produced at \qty{10}{\kilo\volt}, allowing to distinguish between \ac{CH} and \ac{CSH} and between \ac{C3S} and \ac{C2S}.

An \ac{ArBIB} cleaned surface can be imaged at voltages as low as \qty{2.0}{\kilo\volt} (at \qty{0.8}{\nano\ampere}), resulting in an segmentable image.
The microstructure also appears more detailed than in images of the coated surface at \qty{10}{\kilo\volt}.
This is caused by the smaller interaction volume at lower voltages (see \zcref{fig:penetration_depth})
However, the phase contrast between \ac{CH} and \ac{CSH} is diminished compared to the carbon coated specimens at \qty{15}{\kilo\volt}.

The images of the cleaned surfaces suffer from charging artefacts starting at \qty{4.0}{\kilo\volt}, \qty{0.8}{\nano\ampere}.
At \qty{10}{\kilo\volt}, \qty{0.8}{\nano\ampere}, the image becomes unusable.
While current reduction to \qty{50}{\pico\ampere} eliminates the visible charging, the signal becomes very noisy.
Therefore, much longer frame integration times are required, which makes it difficult to obtain sharp details, since images easily become unstable and blurry due to the charging.

Furthermore, the \ac{ArBIB} cleaned surface shows more cracks due to dry-shrinking caused either by the heat from the ion beam or the prolonged vacuum exposure.

If a sample is not coated in the first place, \ac{ArBIB} polishing removes oil residue and reduces scratches as visible in \zcref{fig:mech_polishing_damage}.

\section{\acs*{ArBIB} sectioning}
\label{sec:bib_pol_fixed_results}

\ac{ArBIB} milling allows to section and subsequently image porous specimens without the requirement to embed them in epoxy resin (see \zcref{fig:BSE_ArBIB_CEMI}).
This is especially useful for specimens with pronounced capillary porosity.
Mechanical polishing would damage this not stabilized microstructure and the pores would be filled with debris.

	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{literature/ArBIB-CEMI-BSE.pdf}

       \ac{SEM} image of \qty{28}{\day} hydrated CEM~I.

       The surface was polished using \acl*{ArBIB} and was captured using a through the lens detector in \ac{BSE} mode at \qty{2.0}{\kilo\volt} and \qty{0.1}{\nano\ampere}.
       The contrast was increased in post processing.
    }
    \label{fig:BSE_ArBIB_CEMI}
\end{figure*}

The ion beam milled specimens have to be imaged using low voltages, since the so prepared surface is intrinsically non conductive and a consecutive coating would also alter the microstructure (see \zcref{fig:coating-influence}).
As demonstrated in the previous section, low voltages also result in a limited contrast between phases like \ac{CH} and \ac{CSH} or \ac{C3S} and \ac{C2S}.
However, porosity smaller than large capillary pores are usually still differentiable and therefore segmentable from the solids.

This method of preparation allows a close-to-native imaging of cementitious materials and results in a very flat and clean surface.
This furthermore enables the analysis of very fine porosity, using primarily the \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE} mode \cite{desbois.2014, kleiner.2021a}.

\zcref{fig:csh-arbib-c} shows a needle filled capillary pore.
The needles within the pore are fully visible and show no obvious morphological changes like bending or melting.
However, such a pore cannot be segmented automatically, as not only the material of the cutting plane but also the pore background is visible.

In contrast, the nano porosity shown in \zcref{fig:csh-arbib-d} is segmentable.
Howeverm similar to \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE} images of other porous samples, the segmentation cannot be done using a global threshold value.
While in \ac{BSE} image a rather distinct grey value range represents a single phase, this is not true for \ac{SE} images.
Therefore, the use of local threshold methods can enable a correct segmentation as will be shown in a later chapter.


As mentioned in \zcref{fig:bib_scheme}, an irradiation volume is expected during the \ac{ArBIB} sectioning.
Since hydrate phases are easily damaged by electron radiation as demonstrated in \zcref{fig:ebeam_damage}, the same could be expected during the ion beam preparation.
To determine morphological differences, a CEM~I was sectioned at \num{-140}.
Comparing images of cryo-prepared (\zcref{fig:csh-arbib-SEM}) areas and areas prepared at room temperature (\zcref{fig:csh-arbib-SEM2}) indicate, that there is no visible damage caused by the ion beam without cooling.

Furthermore, the \ac{ArBIB} milling revealed the shape of the cross-section of a \ac{CSH}-needle.
As illustrated in \zcref{fig:csh-arbib-b}, the needles are star-shaped and indeed not hollow as suggested by \textcite{double.1976, double.1978} in the 1970s.

\section{\acs*{ArBIB} preparation for \acl*{NI} combined with \acs*{SEM} - \acs*{BSE} and \acs*{mXRF}}

\Acl{NI} is a technique which requires very flat surfaces \cite{wilson.2018}.
While mechanical polishing works well if done with an oil-based polishing process \cite{gao.2012, wilson.2018}, \ac{ArBIB} sectioning is a promising preparation method of hydraulic cement-based materials, since it does not require water-free resin embedding, even for porous materials, avoids chemical contamination and minimises mechanical damage as demonstrated in the section above.
Furthermore, it avoids the formation of a pronounced relief at the interface of hard (eg. quartz grains) and soft (eg. hydrated cement) materials.
For example, the imaging of microstructures of claystone was challenging until the upcoming of \ac{ArBIB} polishing and milling \cite{yven.2007,houben.2014}.

The objective was to obtain a clean and smooth \ac{ITZ} between the binder and a large quartz grain of a \qty{91}{\day} hydrated CEM~I concrete sample (see \zcref{sec:ni_concrete}).
As shown in \zcref{fig:lm_ni}, an usable area of $\approx$ \qty{7.5}{\milli\meter\squared} was prepared using \ac{ArBIB} sectioning.
The polishing was done at \qty{6}{\kilo\volt}, \qty{2}{\milli\ampere} in multiple sessions for a total of $\approx$ \qty{72}{\hour}.
Between the sessions, the specimen had to be repositioned causing a damaged front area.

To test the surface quality, a height map was measured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (\textit{VK-X3050} by \textsc{Keyence}) with a resolution of \qty{1.373}{\micro\meter\per\px}.
The phase segmentation (quartz-binder) was enabled by an element mapping using \ac{mXRF}.
The quartz grains were segmented based on the subsequent \ce{Si} signal.
Furthermore, a \ac{BSE} image was acquired to find a suitable \ac{ROI} for the \ac{EDX} analysis and the \ac{NI} site.
The 2D datasets were manually aligned by identifying matching points between these datasets using \textit{QGIS 3.42} \cite{QGIS_software}.
\zcref{fig:arbib-experiment-positions} illustrates the position of the different areas measured by these methods.

The raw height map in \zcref{fig:arbib-experiment-positions} was aligned to the dark unpolished surface (compare \zcref{fig:lm_ni}) using three points.
This means, that the surface plane was defined as the zero level by defining three points within this area.
The aligned height map indicates that the ion beam milled the sample at an angle of \qtyrange{5}{6}{\degree} relative to the top surface.

Defining the polished surface as a reference plane using three points reveals the unevenness caused by the three beams (see \zcref{fig:arbib-topography}).
The central area was milled deeper than the lateral areas, creating a V-shaped pattern.
The polished area in the selected \ac{ROI} has a total height difference of approximately \qty{75}{\micro\meter}, whereas local variations are significantly smaller.
This seems to be in accordance to the observations from \textcite{sammer.2024}, where the overall roughness was seemingly worse than in mechanical polishing, while locally smoother surfaces were achieved.

\textcite{ISO25178-2:2022} defines roughness parameters like $S_{dr}$.
The developed area ratio $S_{dr}$ is the percentage increase in surface area due to texture compared to a perfectly flat surface.
It is \num{0} for perfectly smooth surfaces and increases with increasing roughness.
This parameter was calculated within a \qtyproduct{20 x 20}{\px} sliding window for the whole \ac{ROI} (see \zcref{fig:arbib-rough}).
Combined with the segmentation results from the \ac{mXRF} dataset, this allows the surface roughness to be attributed to different phases.

Calculating the mean $S_{dr}$ value for the aggregate area and the binder area results in \qty{59.7(33.5)}{\percent}, and \qty{87.8(41.5)}{\percent} respectively.
In other words, the the quartz grains have smoother surfaces than the cement binder phase.
This can be attributed to the homogeneous composition and the scarcity of phase boundaries (e.g., pores) within the quartz grains, while the binder matrix contains numerous small phase boundaries and incorporates significant porosity.
However, especially at the quartz grains it becomes obvious, that irregularities are causing a shadow effect in the direction of the ion beam.
They cause visible stripes, which are often V-shaped, with the discontinuity at the intersection of the stipes.

Tilted \ac{SEM}-\ac{SE} images illustrate, that there is no visible step between phases of different hardness (see \zcref{fig:arbib-tilt,fig:arbib-tilt-c}).
However, the curtaining artifacts caused by porosity are clearly visible in \zcref{fig:arbib-tilt-b}.
In contrast, \zcref{fig:arbib-tilt-a} shows a smooth surface.
In both cases, the ion beam was from bottom to top.
As indicated in \zcref{fig:arbib-rough}, quartz grains cause a smoother surface and this also seems to be true for the transition from quartz to hydrated cement binder.

Despite using a CEM~I as binder, a slag grain can be identified, which might originate from the \qty{5}{\percent} of allowed other components according to \textcite{dinen197-1.2011}.
The \ac{EDX} mapping derived phase map does not distinguish between \ac{C3S} and \ac{C2S} phases, but divides the hydrate phases granularly into \ac{CSHi} (\ac{HD}), \ac{CASH}, Mg-\ac{CASH}, \ac{CH} and \ac{AFm}/\ac{AFt} phases.
Hydrate phases that could not be further differentiated due to their nano-scale intergrowth and were classified as \ac{CSHo} (\ac{LD}).

The \ac{NI} was done on a $\approx$ \qtyproduct{100 x 325}{\micro\meter\squared} area with \numproduct{20 x 65} indents at a \qty{5}{\micro\meter} spacing.
The \ac{NI} measurements allow to calculate the hardness $H$ and the reduced \textsc{Young}'s modulus $E_r$.
After the indentation process was completed, another \ac{BSE} image was acquired to identify the indents.
Overlaying a grid, as shown in \zcref{fig:BSE-indents}, indicates that the indent positions deviated a few \unit{\micro\meter} from the planned ones.
Furthermore, another \ac{NI} measurement was performed within the same area, resulting in an apparently irregular pattern, making the indent assignment difficult.

Combining the \ac{NI} measurements, the \ac{EDX} phase mapping and the \ac{BSE} image, allows to assign the hardness and the modulus to the different phases.
However, the phase assignment of each of the \num{1140} indents based on the indent positions found in the \ac{BSE} image was unpractical, due to their slight misalignments (see \zcref{fig:ni2D-results}).
Therefore, the high-resolution \ac{EDX} map was subdivided using a grid as indicated in \zcref{fig:BSE-indents, fig:ni2D-results-c,fig:ni2D-results-d}.
Within each grid cell, the are phase composition ratio was calculated.
Grid cells in which no single phase exceeded \qty{50}{\percent} were excluded from the \ac{NI} evaluation.
This resulted in \qty{76.1}{\percent} (\num{868} of \num{1140}) assigned indents within the overlapping area.

The results summarised in \zcref{tab:ni-results} indicate distinct differences between unhydrated and hydrated phases.
Unhydrated materials such as \ac{C3S}/\ac{C2S} and slag exhibit superior mechanical properties.
\ac{C3S}/\ac{C2S} shows a mean hardness of \qty{7.6(3.1)}{\giga\pascal} and a reduced modulus of \qty{92.0(24.1)}{\giga\pascal}, while slag has a hardness of \qty{7.1(1.4)}{\giga\pascal} and a modulus of \qty{81.1(11.4)}{\giga\pascal}.
In contrast, the hydrated phases exhibit much more similar mechanical properties.
\ac{CH} exhibits a mean hardness of \qty{1.8(0.8)}{\giga\pascal} and a modulus of \qty{36.1(9.5)}{\giga\pascal}, which is slightly lower than in \cite{li.2025,li.2026}.
\ac{CSHi} shows values with a hardness of \qty{1.4(1.0)}{\giga\pascal} and a modulus of \qty{29.6(11.9)}{\giga\pascal}, while \ac{CSHo} is slightly lower with a hardness of \qty{1.1(0.5)}{\giga\pascal} and a modulus of \qty{23.5(8.0)}{\giga\pascal}.

\ac{CASH} and \ce{Mg}-\ac{CASH} display comparable properties, with hardness values of \qty{1.2(0.6)}{\giga\pascal} and \qty{1.2(1.3)}{\giga\pascal}, and moduli of \qty{27.4(8.2)}{\giga\pascal} and \qty{28.7(14.8)}{\giga\pascal}, respectively.
The \ac{C3A}/\ac{C4AF} phase is represented by only a single measurement, preventing a reliable evaluation.
Although pores should theoretically yield values of zero, low values are recorded (mean hardness \qty{1.00(1.15)}{\giga\pascal}).
This is likely due to the interaction volume of the indentation including material directly at the crack edges or beneath the shallow pores.
However, these results are still above manually assigned values in \cite{li.2025, li.2026}, where the pores were filled with epoxy resin.
This discrepancy may be attributed to direct contact with the material beneath the pore or at the edges, in contrast to the intermediate contact with epoxy resin, or to imperfect phase assignment.
The quartz aggregate shows the highest hardness of \qty{11.0(2.3)}{\giga\pascal} and a modulus of \qty{84.0(14.0)}{\giga\pascal} in good accordance with \cite{li.2025, li.2026}.

In the literature, hardness values for clinker phases are reported to range from \qtyrange{4.5}{11}{\giga\pascal}, with moduli between \qtyrange{72}{160}{\giga\pascal} \cite{sebastiani.2016,han.2012,xu.2017, lahayne.2022}.
For \ac{CSHi}, hardness values range from \qtyrange{0.9}{1.8}{\giga\pascal} with moduli between \qtyrange{21}{32}{\giga\pascal}, whereas for \ac{CSHo}, hardness values range from \qtyrange{0.3}{1.0}{\giga\pascal} and moduli from \qtyrange{11}{20}{\giga\pascal} \cite{han.2012, xu.2017, lahayne.2022}.
However, these properties depend on the \ac{wb} value; typically, a higher \ac{wb} value results in lower hardness and modulus \cite{xu.2017}.
The values measured in this experiment align with the ranges reported in the literature, although the standard deviations observed here are generally higher than in \cite{sebastiani.2016,xu.2017}.
This might be caused by the different indent assignment, which is usually done in literature by using the deconvolution technique applied to the probability density function of the modulus values \cite{sebastiani.2016,xu.2017}.
A few previous studies only distinguish between \ac{CSHi}, \ac{CSHo}, and \ac{CH}, even when combined with \ac{EDX} measurements \cite{sebastiani.2016, lahayne.2022}.
\textcite{wilson.2018} was additionally able to identify and assigning \ac{NI} measurements to \ac{CASH} phases of a hydrated cement (\ac{wb} = \qty{0.4}) utilising a \ac{EDX} mapping.
They measured hardness values of \qty{1.1(0.35)}{\giga\pascal}, and moduli of \qty{33.5(7.3)}{\giga\pascal}.
However, as shown in this study, the use of detailed \ac{EDX} phase segmentation enables the identification of additional hydrate phases.

\section{Conclusion}

\ac{ArBIB} milling has proven to be a valuable preparation technique for the microstructural characterisation of hydrated cementitious materials.
\ac{ArBIB} polishing effectively removes surface contaminants and mechanical preparation artefacts, enabling high-resolution \ac{BSE} and \ac{SE} imaging at low accelerating voltages without the need for conductive coatings.
However, this approach is limited by potential charging effects and reduced phase contrast compared to high-voltage imaging of coated specimens.
\ac{ArBIB} sectioning offers the distinct advantage of preparing porous samples without resin embedding, thus preserving the native pore structure, especially nano pores.
Furthermore, for micromechanical investigations such as \ac{NI}, \ac{ArBIB} preparation reduces the topographic relief between phases of different hardness, such as aggregates and the cement paste matrix.
Although the technique can introduce specific surface textures like curtaining, correlative microscopy combining \ac{SEM}-\ac{BSE}, \ac{EDX} spectroscopy, and \ac{NI} on \ac{ArBIB}-prepared surfaces successfully facilitated the phase-specific determination of mechanical properties.
This approach highlighted significant differences between anhydrous clinker phases and hydration products, although the precise characterisation of the \ac{ITZ} remained constrained by preparation-induced microcracking.
Furthermore, pores are assigned higher hardness values than in resin embedded specimens, since the nanonindenter will mostly measure pore backgrounds or crack edges.

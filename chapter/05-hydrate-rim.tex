\subsection{Influence of the segmentation method used}

Since the segmentation result is the prerequisite of this method, it can be expected, that it has a large influence on the final result.
Therefore,a section of one image (\SI{14}{\day} hydrated alite, \cite{kleiner.2024a}) was segmented by using three different segmentation methods:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item a manually selected threshold value to distinguish between pores, hydrate phases and the unhydrated clinker,
	\item a manually trained pixel classification algorithm distinguishing between pores, the unhydrated clinker, \ac{CH}, and
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item inner and outer \ac{CSH} (w. \ac{CSHo}),
		\item a combined inner and outer \ac{CSH} (wo. \ac{CSHo}),
	\end{enumerate}
	\item manual segmentation, based on the pixel classification result, ignoring \ac{CSHo}.
\end{enumerate}

%Additionally, a representative area demonstrating the performed measurements is shown in the \ac{sup} of \cite{kleiner.2024}, Figure 2.
Comparing the raw segmentation results (see \ac{sup} of \cite{kleiner.2024}, Figure~1 and Table~\ref{tab:segmentation}), there are obvious differences.
The threshold segmentation leads to many erroneously identified phases.
In contrast, the pixel classification gives a very close result to the manual segmentation.
However, if \ac{CSHo} is taken into account in the pixel classification, the \ac{HLT} distribution and the calculated $l_\text{max}$ are comparable to the threshold-based segmentation, while the data density is significantly reduced (see Table~\ref{tab:segmentation} and Figure~\ref{fig:hlt_segmentation_influence}\cite{kleiner.2024}, \ac{sup}).
This data density reduction is caused by a rough segmentation of the \ac{CSHo} and therefore, a significantly decreased pore space.
Furthermore, a slightly larger 33.3\,\% confidence interval and a significant reduction of the first \ac{HLT} accumulation can be observed, inlcuding the \ac{CSHo}.

Manual segmentation and pixel classification ignoring \ac{CSHo} show nearly identical $l_\text{max}$ values, while restoring the data density (see \cite{kleiner.2024}, \ac{sup}, Figure~3).
The difference of the $l_\text{max}$ of the pixel classification results allows to estimate a thickness of \ac{CSHo} of roughly \SI{0.4}{\micro\meter}.
Nevertheless, segmenting the outer layer presents a non-trivial challenge as it relies on the person performing the segmentation and training the pixel classification.
In many cases, distinguishing whether \ac{CSHo} or merely an artifact such as a flat edge is being segmented remains unclear.

\subsection{Single phase analysis}

Applying the algorithm proposed in section \ref{subsec:algo_hlm} to individually hydrated phases gives the results shown in Figure~\ref{fig:results2d_alite} and \ref{fig:results2d_belite} for alite and belite respectively.
The diagrams consist of a graph indicating frequency of a measured \ac{HLT} in dependency of the grain size (blue-green).
This plot is accompanied by a frequency plot of the grain size diameter (grey) and a grain-size-agnostic distribution plot of the \ac{HLT} frequency (orange).

As expected, smaller particles are more frequent than larger particles throughout all samples tested.
Furthermore, especially larger particles become less frequent with increasing specimen age, which results in a coarser data density.

The \ac{HLT} distributions of alite at 7 to \SI{28}{\day} and belite at \SI{28}{\day} specimens show two distinct accumulations in the \ac{HLT} distribution.
The second, larger accumulation appears at larger \ac{HLT}s, the older the specimen, until it ultimately disappears for really old alite specimens at \SI{84}{\day} and \SI{365}{\day}, where the pore space is mostly consumed by \ac{CSH} and \ac{CH}, which makes the measurement impossible.
However, the \ac{CSH} growth is then also limited by the neighboring particles and hydrate phases, which would also reduce the meaning of the measurement.
The first accumulation is most likely an artifact of the segmentation quality as becomes more obvious in Figure~\ref{fig:hlt_segmentation_influence}.
Furthermore, very small grains seem to have no second \ac{HLT} accumulation, while the first is more pronounced, which also reduced with different segmentation methods.

\subsection{Multi phase analysis}

Applying the algorithm using simple thresholding to mixtures of alite and belite without distinguishing between clinker phases is also possible.
Especially specimens older than 14 or \SI{28}{\day} show two to three distinct \ac{HLT} peaks.
The first peak can be attributed to the erroneous segmentation and might overlap the actual \ac{HLT} for younger specimens.

The raw images of the mixtures (eg. Figure~\ref{fig:hadley}) show, that the \ac{CSH} around belite grows much less/slower, than around alite grains.
This indicates, that the lower \ac{HLT}-peak stems from belite, while the larger one is caused by the \ac{CSH} around alite.
However for younger specimens, the later two peaks are not well distinguishable.
Extracting the peak maxima using Equation~\ref{eqn:fitcurve} between peak-minima, the results shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hyd-rim-mix} can be determined.

Based on the belite results younger than \SI{28}{\day} (see Figure~\ref{fig:results2d_belite}), the belite-\ac{HLT} should be included in the first noise peak and cannot be measured reliably.
By only comparing specimens with an age of \SI{28}{\day} with distinct peaks, a mutual dependence of both phases can be identified.

In Figure~\ref{fig:hyd-rim-mix-28d} it becomes visible, that alite reacts slower with increasing belite content, while belite reacts only slightly faster, if at all.
This uncertainty is caused by the following sources of error:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Overlap/contact of the \ac{CSH} from alite and belite,
	\item and filled Hadley-grains witch no contact to the actual hydrate rim around the grain resulting in an overestimation and lower data density for belite.
\end{itemize}

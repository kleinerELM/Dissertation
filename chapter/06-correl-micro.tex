The contrast in the \ac{BSE} image correlates with the average atomic mass of the phases. Thus, in the \ac{BSE} image of a cement clinker, belite appears darker than the alite (left corner of Figure 2) [67]. Additionally, belite forms large crystals, enabling a visual identification. In contrast, alite appears brighter and consists of smaller crystals (centre and right side of Figure 2). Furthermore, the area contains some pores and MgO (both are black), \ac{C4AF} (lightest grey) and \ac{C3A} (medium grey). 



The material removal resulting from laser ablation is clearly visible in the \ac{BSE} image in Figure 1 and in the \ac{SE} image shown in Figure 3. The \ac{SE} image in Figure 3 was recorded after \ac{LAICPMS} from the same scanning site as in Figure 1. The laser induced surface shows clear signs of melting. The marks may appear different depending on the measuring site as shown in the supplementary data (Figure S-20). Furthermore, the images visualize the width and depth of the interaction of the laser radiation with the material. The exact removal depth is difficult to estimate but seems to be in the range of approximately 7 µm (marking in the lower centre of Figure 3), whereas the width of the \ac{LAICPMS} spots are approximately 6 x 6 µm. It was assumed that the ablation behavior of the different clinker phases varies, but not significantly as indicated by the relatively homogeneous size of the ablation pattern. The line patterns outside the ROI (yellow border in Figure 1; coarse pattern in the centre of Figure 3) originate from pre-ablation, which was performed before the \ac{LAICPMS} measurement.



The Ca, Si, Al and Fe \ac{EDX} element distribution maps located in a subarea of Figure 2 are shown in Figure 4 . As in the \ac{BSE} image, the belite can be seen on the left containing less Ca and more Si as the alite on the right. In between \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF} are present as documented by the Fe and Al element distribution maps (Figure 4). Thus, all major phases of OPC clinker are present in the mapped area. Mappings of elements like Mg, S (minor elements in clinker) also illustrate the position of phases like K2SO4 and MgO (Figure 4). The same is true for C and Cl which are elements contained in the embedding material (epoxy resin). In contrast, the maps of Na and Ti are very noisy and difficult to interpret. These mappings can be found in Figure S-2. A full phase map was calculated using the software Aztec 5.0 and the result is shown in Figure 5. Comparison with the Si element distribution map in Figure 4 reveals, that the phase map distinguished alite, belite, MgO and alkali sulphates quite well. \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF} are shown in one color, the reason is, that it was expected, that the phase size of \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF} is too small to be distinguished by \ac{LAICPMS} .


The elemental maps of Cr, Cu, Mn and P are also very noisy and therefore are not shown here. Generally, these elemental maps can be obtained in good quality, if the X-ray count number of the respective mappings would be increased. However, the \ac{EDX} quality was not the major focus of this study. 
Figure 6 shows a comparison of the chemical analysis presented in Table 1 and the mean result of three \ac{EDX} mappings generated with the same \ac{EDX} detector. Values below 0.1 wt.-\% oxide are below the detection limit of \ac{EDX}. Obviously, the oxide concentrations shown in Figure 6 show some differences and the standard deviation determined from three mappings is in the range of the differences. This indicates that total analyzed \ac{SEM} area is slightly too small to be representative for the chemical composition of the clinker. 


Results in Figure 7 show the element distribution maps obtained by \ac{LAICPMS} . Obviously compared to \ac{EDX} element distribution maps shown in Figure 4, the \ac{LAICPMS} maps possess a lower spatial resolution. When the Al element distribution maps obtained by both methods are compared (Figure 4 and Figure 7), it is revealed that \ac{LAICPMS} is not able to sharply represent the phase boundaries between the finely grained \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF} phases and the silicate phases. Therefore, in the following \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF} are not differentiated and described as interstitial phase. However, the differences in the element distribution between alite and belite are very noticeable. I.e., the Ba, Rb and V mappings show a significant concentration variation in both phases. Nevertheless, for the interstitial phase (\ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF}) an enrichment of Mn is seen in Figure 7. E.g., the Mn and Al mappings (Al and Mn) depict their highest concentrations in the same areas. In addition, some elements show regions of very high concentrations, which are called hotspots in this article. Rb and K hotspots seem to appear in the same place. Thus, it can be assumed that both alkali metals are enriched in the same phase. As shown by \ac{EDX} element and deduced phase maps (Figure 4, Figure 5 and Figure S 2), K is associated with S. Therefore, it is concluded that Rb is incorporated in K2SO4. This may be due to the equivalent ionic character (+1). However, also Cr, V, Zn seem to be prominent in the same hotspots, despite of their highly different atom size and oxidation number.

In contrast to \ac{EDX}, \ac{LAICPMS} has lower detection limits for most of the elements. However, the spatial resolution is too low to exactly quantify concentrations in phases that are in the size of the laser ablation spot size, such as \ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF}.
Nevertheless, by combining the phase segmentation, acquired using the \ac{EDX}-mapping data, with the \ac{LAICPMS} data (Figure 7), the main clinker phases alite and belite and interstitial phases like \ac{C4AF} and \ac{C3A} may be analyzed, even though this analysis may be inaccurate due to several reasons, which are discussed in the following. Comparison of \ac{EDX} and \ac{LAICPMS} quantification results for the interstitial phase are shown in Figure 8. For example, the Al content measured by \ac{EDX} and \ac{LAICPMS} -mappings at the same locations varies significantly, e.g. \ac{EDX} 23.1 wt.-\% and \ac{LAICPMS} 7.8 ± 4.2 wt.-\%. The lower value for \ac{LAICPMS} might origin from inaccurate registration of both data sets, inaccurate calibration or most probably the phase variation due to the z-effect. Nevertheless, obtained concentrations (Al, Mg, Ti, K, Na, Mn, Figure 8) always have the same order of magnitude. Therefore, it can be assumed that the registration result is reliable. For the same area the presence of the following trace elements (i.e. < 0.1 wt.-\%) could be proven by \ac{LAICPMS} : Ba, Zn, Cr, V, Rb (Figure 7 and Figure 8). The quantified concentrations are similar for all analyzed clinker areas (see supplementary document). For area 3 (Figure S-22) also the Si concentration of the interstitial phase was measured by \ac{LAICPMS} . Results show that by \ac{EDX} the Si content is lower, and the Al content is higher than concentrations given by \ac{LAICPMS} (Figure S-22). This can be seen as hint that due to the larger spot size the laser ablation results are more deviated by neighbouring silicate phases than \ac{EDX} analysis. Reversely, this results in the observedrved low Al concentration (Figure 8 and Figure S-22). 
These findings proof that the laser spot size is too large to quantitatively analyse the interstitial phases. Nevertheless, enrichment of for example Mn in the interstitial phase can be proven by \ac{LAICPMS} . Furthermore, an inaccurate image registration seems to be a minor problem, as the same trends in major, minor and trace element concentration can be found in all investigated areas (compare Tables S-1 to S-6).

\section{LA-ICP-MS spot analysis}

In addition to \ac{LAICPMS} and \ac{EDX} mapping data, spot analyses have been collected on sites that were selected based on the \ac{EDX} element distribution maps. The prerequisite for selecting sites for spot analyses was that the area should be large enough to ensure that the laser spot ablates only a single phase. Therefore, the LA spots are re-allocated on the \ac{BSE} image and the \ac{EDX} phase map (Figure 9). Obviously, the spots for belite were more successfully placed in large homogeneous areas than for alite. The reason is the smaller size of alite crystals. 

For the spot analysis, more elements could be measured as the signal to noise ratio is drastically increased when the spot size is enlarged from approximately 36 µm² (square shape) to 415 µm² (round shape). Thus, Pb, Ni, As, Sr, Cu and P could be measured additionally in the spot analysis. However, other trace elements like Hg and Sb still cannot be measured because of their extremely low concentration even if it would be possible to further enlarge the spot size to improve the detection limit.

There are rather large deviations between mapping and spot analysis for most of the elements in alite and belite (Figure 10 and Figure 11). Besides different calibration methods, another reason for this deviation is most likely the effect that during spot ablation more area from neighbouring phases is analysed than in mapping mode (larger spot size). Furthermore, the \ac{LAICPMS} results are further deviated due to the z-effect, e.g. by material that is ablated from a certain depth. This z-effect is also occurring in \ac{EDX} analysis but for the used 12 kV acceleration voltage the excitation volume diameter is not larger than 1.6 - 2.0 µm, depending on the material. Therefore, both \ac{EDX} and \ac{LAICPMS} are often affected by neighbouring phases in 3 dimensions, if the phase size is similar or smaller than the analysed sample volume. Figure 10 and Figure 11 show the quantitative element concentrations for alite and belite obtained from segmented mappings (given in wt.-\% oxide) and from spot analyses. The following element concentrations were determined by both methods (Al, Mg, Ti, K, Na, Mn). The Al content in alite, as documented by results in Figure 10, as determined by \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} spot analysis is 1.5 ± 0.4 wt.-\%, by \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} mapping 1.5 ± 0.0 wt.-\% and by \ac{LAICPMS} mapping 4.30 ± 2.88 wt.-\% respectively \ac{LAICPMS} spot analysis: 2.10 ± 0.74 wt.-\%. The deviation between \ac{EDX} and \ac{LAICPMS} seems rather large and is probably mainly caused by the effect of neighbouring phases, e.g. more Al from interstitial phase is detected by the \ac{LAICPMS} analysis.

However, the spot analysis re \ac{SEM} bles the results shown by the \ac{LAICPMS} -mappings, both results reveal the accumulation of trace elements in clinker phases. Based on the LA ICP MS mappings it was shown that alite contains more Ba, V and Rb. This is confirmed by spot analyses for Ba and V but not for Rb (Figure 10 and Figure 11). But standard deviation of Rb and most other element concentrations determined by \ac{LAICPMS} is larger for alite than for belite. This indicates that alite concentrations are, due to smaller crystal sizes, more affected by neighbouring phases (e.g. hotspots that contain Rb in high concentration) than belite. 

Barium is taken as an example to furthermore interpret the data of the mapped images and the spot analysis. In the upper elemental distribution maps, it is clearly visible that Ba is enriched in belite (Figure 7). Additionally, some local spots of higher concentrations can be found between the alite grains, possibly in the interstitial phases (Figure 7). The mapping data shown in Figure 10 and Figure 11 underline the \ac{SEM} iquantitative observation documented in Figure 7. For alite, the determined \ac{LAICPMS} Ba concentrations are 0.0187 ± 0.0113 wt.-\% (mapping) and 0.0089 ± 0.0040 wt.-\% (spot analysis). The belite shows higher Ba concentrations, e.g. 0.0351 ± 0.0145 wt.-\% (mapping) and 0.0291 ± 0.0019 wt.-\% (spot analysis). Thus, 2-times (mapping) and 3-times (spot) higher Ba concentrations are found in belite than in alite. Ba is also detected in areas of the interstitial phase, e.g. together with high Al content (mapping, Figure 7 and Figure 8). For the interstitial phase (\ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF}) the Ba concentration is between that of alite and belite (0.0234 ± 0.0139 wt.-\%). But as seen from mapping data in Figure 7 the Ba in the interstitial phase is not as homogeneously distributed as in belite. A more detailed analysis is necessary to reveal the incorporation of Ba in the interstitial phase. Here it can be stated that also heterogenous distributions of trace elements in clinker phases can occur.

For major and minor phases, a comparison to \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} data shows the limited accuracy of \ac{LAICPMS} . Therefore, a combination with \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} should be considered if major and minor elements are of interest.
In a second and third dataset, at a different area on the same polished clinker grain, the described method combination of \ac{LAICPMS} and \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} was repeated. All data can be found in the supplementary document. Therein, the same results and tendencies can be found as above described. The enrichment of elements in alite, belite and the interstitial phase (\ac{C3A} and \ac{C4AF}) measured wit \ac{LAICPMS} is visualized in Figure 12. The results demonstrate the repeatability of the described phase segmentation method for the \ac{LAICPMS} dataset based on the \ac{SEM} -\ac{EDX} data.

Furthermore, while smaller phases could not be reliably segmented in all datasets, the enrichment of Cr and Rb in the water-soluble potassium sulphate phases is still visible in the first and the second measuring site.
